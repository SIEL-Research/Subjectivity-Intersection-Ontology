# 5.5 Process Ontology and Phenomenology

## Introduction

Several influential movements in twentieth-century philosophy rejected the assumption that reality is best understood as a collection of static, self-contained substances. Process philosophers, phenomenologists, and related traditions developed accounts in which relation, temporality, embodiment, and development are constitutive rather than secondary. These approaches did not converge upon a single process ontology, but they substantially altered the conceptual terms in which subjectivity and world could be related.

Nishida Kitarō, Alfred North Whitehead, and Maurice Merleau-Ponty each proposed an influential alternative to isolated-substance models of subjectivity. Nishida situated self and world within the self-determining logic of place. Whitehead analyzed actuality through event-like occasions constituted by prehensive relations. Merleau-Ponty treated embodied perception as an intertwining of body, others, and world. Their methods, vocabularies, and philosophical aims remain distinct.

These traditions therefore provide important historical comparisons for Subjectivity Intersection Ontology (SIO), the framework introduced in this paper. The comparison is limited to structural similarities and differences. It does not imply historical dependence, and it does not treat the terms “place,” “actual occasion,” or “Flesh” as equivalents of SIO's third subjectivity.

## 5.5.1 Nishida Kitarō

### Historical Background

Nishida Kitarō (1870–1945) is generally regarded as the founding figure of what later came to be known as the Kyoto School. His philosophy developed through several stages rather than presenting one unchanged system. Early work centered on pure experience and self-awareness. From the mid-1920s onward, Nishida increasingly articulated a “logic of place” (*basho no ronri*), and his later writings situated individual action and historical reality within a dialectical world.

The concept of *basho*, or place, was introduced to reconsider the relation among judgments, universals, consciousness, and reality. A place is not simply a spatial container in which independently constituted entities happen to be located. It is the field or context within which determinations can appear as what they are. Nishida described progressively more inclusive places and identified the ultimate place as absolute nothingness (*zettai mu*).

Absolute nothingness should not be understood as a nihilistic absence or as one entity behind other entities. It is intended to name a place that cannot itself be captured as a determinate object and that includes the opposition between being and non-being, subject and object, and universal and particular. Nishida's formulations changed over time and are not free of interpretive difficulty. The concept nevertheless provides the basis for his account of a world that determines itself without requiring an external substantial ground.

### Ontological Structure

Nishida's logic of place assigns ontological priority to the field within which self and world are differentiated and related. Neither an isolated subject nor a self-sufficient object serves as the final point of departure. Subjective and objective determinations arise within a more encompassing place that cannot be reduced to either pole.

Self-determination is central to this account. Nishida describes reality as the self-determination of place or, in later formulations, of absolute nothingness. This language does not posit a separate agent that first exists and then performs the act of determination. It attempts to describe how determinate beings and events emerge within a ground that is active without being a determinate substance.

The self is correspondingly non-substantial. It becomes concrete through action, expression, self-negation, and relations with the world and other persons. Nishida's later analyses of the historical world emphasize reciprocal determination: individuals form the world through their actions, while the historical world supplies the conditions under which individuals act and understand themselves. The one and the many are therefore neither simply identical nor externally juxtaposed.

This dynamic relation is sometimes expressed as the self-identity of absolute contradictories. The formulation is intended to preserve opposition within unity rather than eliminate difference. It gives Nishida's ontology a developmental and relational structure, although the exact status of contradiction, nothingness, and individual agency remains contested in the scholarship.

### Relation to SIO

Nishida and SIO both treat subjectivity as relational, dynamic, and irreducible to an isolated substance. Nishida's account of individuals and the historical world as mutually determining provides a partial comparison to recursive reconstruction. His insistence that self and world become intelligible only within a more encompassing relational field also approaches SIO's rejection of merely external relation.

The generative logic is nevertheless different. Nishida's development proceeds through the logic and self-determination of place. Differentiated individuals interact within a historical world, but the resulting field is not clearly described as an irreducible third subjectivity generated by the constitutive intersection of two subjects. Absolute nothingness is neither a subject produced by their encounter nor a third term alongside them. Nishida therefore approaches N1–N3 in partial and interpretation-dependent respects without formulating the complete SIO structure.

## 5.5.2 Alfred North Whitehead

### Historical Background

Alfred North Whitehead (1861–1947) developed his mature process philosophy in works including *Science and the Modern World* and *Process and Reality*. His “philosophy of organism” replaces the traditional priority of enduring material substance with an account of actuality as becoming. The basic units of this account are actual entities, also called actual occasions.

An actual occasion is not a substance persisting unchanged through time. It is a temporally structured process of becoming that achieves a determinate unity and then perishes as an immediate subject. Enduring things are analyzed as organized societies or sequences of such occasions rather than as independent substrates that remain identical beneath all change.

Prehension names the manner in which an actual occasion takes account of, feels, or incorporates aspects of prior actuality. The term does not necessarily imply conscious perception. It designates the relational inclusion of data within the becoming of a new occasion. Creativity names the general principle through which the many inherited data become one determinate actuality and that new unity, in turn, enters the many from which subsequent occasions arise.

### Ontological Structure

Whitehead's ontology is processual and event-centered. Reality consists fundamentally of acts of becoming rather than static substances. Each actual occasion arises through a process of concrescence: a multiplicity of inherited data is integrated into a novel unity. The occasion is therefore constituted through its relations to an antecedent world. Relation is internal to actuality, not an external connection added after an entity has become what it is.

The completed occasion has both a subjective and an objective aspect. During concrescence it is a subject integrating its prehensions; upon completion it becomes an objective datum available to successor occasions. This transition gives process an irreversible temporal direction. The past conditions the present, and the completed present contributes to the conditions of the future.

Actual occasions are discrete, but they are not independent in the manner of windowless units. Their determinate identities depend upon selective relations to prior occasions. Larger-scale continuities—including organisms, persons, and material objects—are organized societies of occasions with inherited patterns. Whitehead thus combines event pluralism with pervasive relational dependence.

Process is ontologically primary in this scheme. Stability is real, but it is explained through patterned repetition and inheritance across occasions rather than through an unchanging substantial bearer. Novelty likewise arises within relational concrescence rather than being introduced into an otherwise completed world from outside the process.

### Relation to SIO

Whitehead represents one of the closest process-ontological comparisons to SIO. An actual occasion is constituted through relations, integrates a multiplicity into a novel unity, possesses a form of subjective immediacy, and subsequently conditions further becoming. These features supply partial structural analogues to constitutive intersection, emergence, and recursive influence.

The differences remain decisive for the N1–N6 assessment. Concrescence is not generally an intersection between two differentiated subjectivities. It integrates a multiplicity of data derived from prior actual occasions, and those data need not be subjects in the sense employed by SIO. The resulting occasion is a new subject of experience in Whitehead's technical sense, but it is not explicitly formulated as an irreducible third subjectivity arising between two subjects.

Whiteheadian inheritance is also temporally asymmetric. Once an actual occasion has become, it conditions its successors; a later occasion does not alter the completed becoming of its antecedents. Organized societies may be reconfigured through ongoing feedback among successor processes, but this is not identical to an emergent third subjectivity recursively reconstructing the original subjects. Whitehead therefore displays substantial proximity to N1–N3 while stopping short of N6 as defined in this paper.

## 5.5.3 Maurice Merleau-Ponty

### Historical Background

Maurice Merleau-Ponty (1908–1961) developed a phenomenology centered on embodiment, perception, expression, and intersubjectivity. In *The Structure of Behavior* and *Phenomenology of Perception*, he challenged both mechanistic accounts of the body and intellectualist accounts that make perception the product of a detached constituting consciousness. Perception is instead rooted in the lived body's practical involvement with its environment.

The body is not only an object among objects. It is also the embodied subject through which a world is perceived and acted within. Its capacities, habits, orientation, and motility structure the field of possible experience before reflective judgment. Subjectivity is therefore situated and bodily rather than a self-transparent mental substance.

Merleau-Ponty's later, unfinished work, especially *The Visible and the Invisible*, introduced the concepts of Flesh and chiasm to deepen this account. These concepts do not replace his earlier phenomenology with a fully completed metaphysical system. They indicate a later ontological direction in which sensing and sensed, body and world, and self and other are understood through reversibility and intertwining.

### Ontological Structure

Reciprocal perception is fundamental to Merleau-Ponty's account. The body sees and can be seen, touches and can be touched. Its activity and receptivity cannot be separated into two independent orders. The familiar example of one hand touching the other shows that the roles of touching and touched are reversible without becoming strictly identical at the same instant.

Flesh names the common element or relational field within which this reversibility is possible. It is not material substance in the conventional sense, not a collective mind, and not an entity located between subject and object. It expresses their shared belonging to a sensible world in which the perceiver is also perceptible. The chiasm designates the crossing or intertwining of these dimensions.

Intercorporeality extends this structure to relations among embodied subjects. Because bodies inhabit a common sensible field and share structures of action and perception, another person's gestures can be understood as meaningful without first being inferred from an inaccessible interior state. Difference is retained: reversibility does not imply the fusion of perspectives or complete access to another's experience.

The world is consequently a relational field rather than a neutral container facing an observing subject. Perception emerges through the ongoing adjustment between bodily capacities and environmental solicitations. Meaning is neither wholly imposed by the subject nor fully constituted in advance within the object.

This account has influenced later work on embodied cognition and enactivism. Enactive approaches differ among themselves, but many draw upon Merleau-Ponty's analysis of the lived body and his rejection of a detached, representational mind. That influence concerns embodied activity and world-involvement; it does not make enactivism a direct continuation of every aspect of his later ontology.

### Relation to SIO

Merleau-Ponty develops one of the most extensive relational phenomenologies of the twentieth century. His accounts of embodiment, intercorporeality, reversibility, and Flesh make subjectivity inseparable from bodily and worldly relations. These features provide substantial comparisons to SIO's emphasis on differentiated perspectives and constitutive relation.

Flesh, however, is not an irreducible third subjectivity generated by the intersection of two differentiated subjects. It is the ontological element or field that makes their intertwining possible. Intercorporeal encounter may alter perception, action, and self-understanding, but Merleau-Ponty does not explicitly assign the relation itself the status of a new subject that recursively reconstitutes both participants.

The structural proximity is therefore limited. Merleau-Ponty offers strong resources for N2 and for the relational conditions surrounding N1, but his ontology does not clearly satisfy the third-subject and recursive-reconstruction requirements as defined by SIO. The comparison remains phenomenological and ontological without establishing equivalence.

## Interim Verdict

Nishida, Whitehead, and Merleau-Ponty each replace substance-centered explanation with dynamic relational structures. Nishida situates self and world within the self-determining logic of place. Whitehead makes process and prehensive relation fundamental to actual occasions. Merleau-Ponty understands embodied subjectivity through perception, intercorporeality, and the intertwining of Flesh.

Their work substantially narrows the conceptual distance to SIO. Each framework gives constitutive significance to relation, rejects an isolated-subject model, and explains actuality through processes of determination or becoming. Whitehead's concrescence is especially close to an account of relation-generated novelty, while Nishida and Merleau-Ponty provide developed accounts of relational subjectivity and field-like ontological conditions.

None of the three, however, explicitly formulates the complete conjunction of:

- differentiated subjectivities;
- constitutive intersection;
- an irreducible emergent third subjectivity; and
- recursive reconstruction of the originating subjectivities.

They are accordingly treated in this paper as major historical predecessors rather than complete formulations of SIO. This verdict is confined to the structural criteria defined in Section 4 and does not rank the philosophical adequacy of the positions considered.
