3. Comparative Ontological Methodology

## 3.1 Why Comparison Requires Methodology

Ontological comparison cannot be conducted simply by identifying shared terms. Across philosophical traditions, the same term may designate different concepts, while different terms may express similar structures. Translation may also create an appearance of lexical agreement even when the underlying ontological assumptions, relations among components, direction of constitution, and understanding of reality differ substantially.

For example, two theories may both emphasize “relation,” yet one may understand relation as an external connection between already existing entities, while the other treats relation as a condition through which the relata themselves are constituted. Similarly, the presence of a “third term” does not establish that the third is a subjectivity, that it is generated through the intersection of two subjectivities, or that it acts back upon the subjectivities from which it originates.

Ontological comparison therefore requires an explicit method for distinguishing lexical agreement, conceptual similarity, structural correspondence, and ontological equivalence. If the criteria remain implicit, judgments of similarity and difference may shift with the selection of comparison cases, terminological translation, or interpretive assumptions.

The methodology developed in this chapter is not proposed as a universal taxonomy of ontologies. It is introduced specifically as a framework for evaluating structural correspondence with Subjectivity Intersection Ontology (SIO).

Nor is this methodology intended to replace historical interpretation with a mechanical procedure. The reconstruction of a theory in its linguistic, historical, and argumentative context remains indispensable. The purpose of this chapter is to make the grounds of the structural judgment that follows such interpretation explicit, transparent, and open to examination.

## 3.2 The Problem of Superficial Similarity

Ontological comparison is vulnerable to treating lexical resemblance as structural resemblance. Terms such as “triad,” “emergence,” “relation,” “subjectivity,” “unity,” and “generation” occur in diverse philosophical contexts and do not possess uniform meanings.

Not every theory with a triadic structure proposes the same ontology. A third term may be assigned the status of mediation, synthesis, symbol, relation, community, field, process, or observer-dependent description. The mere presence of a third term does not establish that it is an irreducible subjectivity.

Likewise, the use of the term “emergence” does not determine what emerges, from what it emerges, or according to what direction of constitution it arises. The emergent term may be an observer’s descriptive construct, a functional organization, a psychological experience, or a subjectivity treated as ontologically real. These possibilities must be distinguished.

The same applies to relation. A relation that connects already existing terms is not equivalent to a relation through which the terms themselves are constituted. Nor can self, person, consciousness, perspective, epistemic subject, legal subject, and grammatical subject be assumed to possess the same ontological status.

This study therefore rejects the following inferences:

```text
Shared vocabulary → identical concept
Identical concept → identical structure
Triadic form → identical ontology
Functional similarity → identical ontological status
Similar consequence → identical generative principle
```

Terminological agreement may provide a starting point for comparison, but it is not itself evidence of structural equivalence. The relevant question is not whether the same words are used, but what constitutes what, which status is assigned to each component, and how the relation as a whole is defined.

## 3.3 Levels of Comparison

This study distinguishes six levels of ontological comparison. The purpose of this distinction is to prevent similarity at one level from being treated as sufficient evidence of equivalence at another.

### 3.3.1 Vocabulary

The vocabulary level identifies whether identical or similar terms are used. These may include subjectivity, relation, intersection, third term, generation, emergence, unity, and closure.

Lexical agreement may assist in identifying relevant comparison cases. Because the same word may express different concepts, however, this level alone cannot support a conclusion concerning structural correspondence.

### 3.3.2 Concept

The conceptual level examines the meaning, scope, and theoretical role of the terms employed.

For example, the conceptual content of a third term differs depending on whether it mediates two terms, synthesizes them, or is itself a subjectivity. Conceptual analysis therefore moves beyond verbal resemblance to reconstruct the role that each concept performs within the theory under examination.

### 3.3.3 Structure

The structural level compares the arrangement, differentiation, dependence, and direction of constitution among concepts.

Even when two theories contain similar concepts, they do not share the same structure if the relations among those concepts differ. Structural comparison examines which terms are presupposed, which terms are constituted through relation, and which terms function as conditions for the constitution of others.

### 3.3.4 Ontology

The ontological level compares the kind of reality attributed to each component.

A third term treated as a psychological experience, epistemic construction, social description, linguistic form, or metaphor cannot be identified without qualification with a third term treated as an ontological subjectivity. Functional or descriptive similarity does not establish ontological equivalence when the relevant components are assigned different ontological statuses.

### 3.3.5 Operation

The operational level compares how components are generated, act upon one another, and alter the relation as a whole.

This level distinguishes the origin of generation, the constitution of intersection, the direction of constitution, the emergence of the third term, and the action of the third upon the originating terms. In comparisons with SIO, the question of what constitutes what is particularly important.

### 3.3.6 Empirical Consequence

The level of empirical consequence compares the observable outcomes that may be predicted by each theory.

Different ontologies may predict similar forms of synchronization, coordination, mutual transformation, or co-generation. Similar empirical consequences do not, however, establish that the theories adopt the same ontological structure. Conversely, structurally similar ontologies need not immediately yield identical empirical predictions.

The six levels are therefore distinguished as follows:

```text
Lexical similarity ≠ conceptual equivalence
Conceptual similarity ≠ structural equivalence
Structural similarity ≠ ontological equivalence
Operational similarity ≠ empirical confirmation
```

This distinction makes it possible to state separately what corresponds and what does not correspond at each stage of the comparison.

## 3.4 Structural Evaluation Criteria

This study organizes the structure of SIO defined in Chapter 2 into six explicit criteria, designated N1–N6. These criteria are not intended to score the philosophical value, historical importance, or truth of the theories under comparison. Their purpose is to examine, through a common framework, the extent to which a compared theory corresponds structurally with SIO.

| Criterion | Evaluates |
|---|---|
| N1 | Subjective status of the candidate third term |
| N2 | Direction of generation through intersection |
| N3 | Action upon and reconstitution of the originating subjectivities |
| N4 | Ontological status of the candidate third term |
| N5 | Generality and theoretical scope of the structure |
| N6 | Integration of N1–N5 within a single ontology |

N1–N6 are not additive scores. N1–N5 are structural criteria assessed separately, while N6 is not an additional scoring item but a conjunction condition for determining whether the required components are integrated within a single theory.

### 3.4.1 N1—Irreducible Third Subjectivity

Does the compared theory posit a third term related to two subjectivities and treat that term as a subjectivity irreducible to their sum, average, metaphor, abstraction, functional device, or observer-dependent description?

The presence of a third term alone does not satisfy N1. The third must be assigned the status of subjectivity and must be irreducible to either originating subjectivity.

### 3.4.2 N2—Generation Through Intersection

Is the third subjectivity defined not as something existing independently prior to the relation, but as something generated through the constitutive intersection of differentiated subjectivities?

A structure in which two subjectivities are mediated by a pre-existing third term, or integrated into a pre-existing whole, does not by itself satisfy N2. The question is whether the third subjectivity is constituted through the intersection itself.

### 3.4.3 N3—Reconstitution of the Originating Subjectivities

Does the constituted third subjectivity act back upon the subjectivities from which it originates and transform, reorganize, or reconstitute their perspectives, relations, actions, or modes of existence?

N3 is not satisfied if the third subjectivity remains only a static result. The constitution of the third must alter the originating subjectivities in some relevant respect.

### 3.4.4 N4—Ontological Reality

Is the candidate third term treated as an ontological reality rather than being confined to an epistemological, psychological, sociological, linguistic, or metaphorical construct?

N4 does not assess whether the ontological claim made by the theory is true. It assesses the ontological status that the compared theory itself assigns to the candidate third term.

N4 is independent of N1 and N2. Satisfaction of N4 does not establish that the candidate third term is a subjectivity in the sense defined by SIO or that it is generated through intersection. Whether the candidate third term has subjective status is assessed under N1, while whether its direction of generation proceeds through intersection is assessed under N2.

### 3.4.5 N5—General Theoretical Scope

Is the proposed structure formulated as a principle whose scope extends beyond a particular person, dyad, community, religion, culture, or exceptional case, and potentially to subjectivity and existence more generally?

A structure resembling SIO within a restricted domain may remain significant for comparison. If it is not formulated as a general ontological principle, however, that resemblance must be distinguished from satisfaction of N5.

### 3.4.6 N6—Complete Structural Conjunction

Does a single theory satisfy N1–N5 and integrate them within a self-consistent general ontology?

- differentiated subjectivities and an irreducible third subjectivity;
- generation of the third subjectivity through constitutive intersection;
- action of the third subjectivity upon and reconstitution of the originating subjectivities;
- ontological reality of the candidate third term; and
- generality and theoretical scope of the integrated structure.

N6 is not satisfied merely because some of these components are present. Nor can it be satisfied by collecting separate components from different periods, texts, schools, or mutually incompatible interpretations. N1–N5 must be integrated within what can defensibly be reconstructed as a single general ontology.

N6 is not an independent score added to N1–N5. It is a conjunction condition for determining whether the complete ontological structure of SIO is present as an integrated structure within the compared theory. N6 receives clear satisfaction only when N1–N5 are each clearly satisfied and integrated within a single ontology. If any of N1–N5 is not satisfied, N6 is not satisfied. Partial or interpretation-dependent satisfaction is appropriate only where N1–N5 are each either clearly or partially satisfied, none is judged not satisfied, and the satisfaction or integration of at least one component remains interpretation-dependent. Insufficient evidence is recorded where one or more of N1–N5 cannot be determined from the available sources and no independently established non-satisfaction already decides the result.

## 3.5 Structural Equivalence

The principal unit of comparison adopted in this study is structure rather than vocabulary. Structural equivalence is not established merely because two theories employ the same terms or address similar themes.

An assessment of structural equivalence with SIO must compare at least the following elements:

- the terms constituting the theory;
- the differentiation among those terms;
- the ontological status of each term;
- the direction of constitution;
- the conditions under which the relation is constituted;
- the mode through which the third subjectivity is generated;
- the action of the third subjectivity upon the originating subjectivities; and
- the theoretical scope of the structure.

The direction of constitution is particularly important. Two theories may contain the same number of terms, yet differ structurally if the third term precedes the other two in one theory but is constituted through their intersection in the other. A structure in which the third term unilaterally constitutes the other two also differs from one in which two terms constitute a third that subsequently acts back upon them.

A triadic organization alone therefore does not establish structural equivalence with SIO. The status, dependencies, and direction of constitution of the respective terms must also correspond.

Structural equivalence does not mean that two theories are identical in every respect. It refers to correspondence with the central structure of SIO along the dimensions evaluated in this study. Differences in historical context, terminology, purpose, domain of application, or argumentative form are described separately from structural correspondence.

For the purposes of this study, a judgment of clear structural equivalence with SIO requires clear satisfaction of N1–N5 and clear satisfaction of N6 as their conjunction condition. Clear satisfaction of N6 is therefore a necessary condition for identifying clear structural equivalence. It does not, however, establish historical identity, intellectual influence, conceptual identity in every respect, or the truth of the ontology under comparison.

## 3.6 Boundaries of Structural Assessment

The presence of elements shared with SIO does not by itself establish that the compared theory is the same ontology. This study distinguishes similarity, partial correspondence, structural equivalence, and historical identity.

```text
Shared vocabulary ≠ shared ontology
Triadic form ≠ structural equivalence
Functional similarity ≠ ontological identity
Historical priority ≠ complete anticipation of SIO
Structural equivalence ≠ historical influence
```

A theory’s satisfaction of some of N1–N5 may indicate an important structural similarity. Partial satisfaction must nevertheless be distinguished from equivalence with the complete structure of SIO.

For example, a theory may recognize a third term without treating it as generated through intersection, in which case it does not satisfy N2. A theory may emphasize constitution through relation without attributing subjectivity or ontological reality to the relation itself, in which case it does not satisfy N1 or N4. The originating subjectivities may also undergo change without that change being defined as the action of a third subjectivity, in which case satisfaction of N3 is limited.

This study restricts criterion-level judgments to four categories:

| Assessment | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Clear satisfaction | The structure corresponding to the criterion is explicitly identifiable within the compared theory. |
| Partial or interpretation-dependent satisfaction | Some structural correspondence is present, but its scope, ontological status, or interpretation remains uncertain. |
| Non-satisfaction | The corresponding structure is absent, or the compared theory explicitly proposes a different structure. |
| Insufficient evidence | The available sources or interpretations do not permit a determination. |

These assessments are neither quantified nor added together, and they are not consolidated into a single grade. Insufficient evidence is not equivalent to non-satisfaction, and partial satisfaction is not merely a weaker expression of clear satisfaction. Each evaluation must specify what corresponds, what is absent, and what remains dependent upon interpretation.

The **SIO Requirement** column identifies the requirement corresponding to each criterion within the structure defined in Chapter 2.

The **Textual Evidence** column records passages from the primary sources of the compared theory and, where necessary, the bibliographic information required to locate them. As far as possible, this column reports what the compared theory states without anticipating the subsequent structural judgment.

The **Interpretive Reconstruction** column explains how the textual evidence is understood as a structure within the theory’s own terminology, historical context, and internal relations. Where interpretation is contested, the principal alternatives and the grounds for the reconstruction adopted in this study are stated.

The **Structural Assessment** column compares the reconstructed structure with N1–N6 and records clear satisfaction, partial or interpretation-dependent satisfaction, non-satisfaction, or insufficient evidence. Relevant qualifications are reported together with the assessment.

This three-stage separation distinguishes objections concerning textual evidence from objections concerning the reconstruction of a theory and from objections concerning the application of the structural criteria. A reader may dispute one stage without necessarily rejecting the other stages or the comparative method as a whole.

Cross-case judgments concerning structural correspondence and historical novelty are reserved for the comparative synthesis in Chapter 5, where they are derived from the criterion-level evidence rather than assigned within individual comparisons.

## 3.8 Methodological Scope

The framework developed in this chapter is presented as one possible method for comparative ontology. It is neither the only legitimate form of comparison nor a replacement for intellectual history, genealogy, philology, conceptual history, comparative phenomenology, or contextualist inquiry. Different research questions require different comparative criteria.

N1–N6 are not neutral and universal measures for evaluating ontology in general. They are intentionally SIO-relative criteria constructed to examine structural correspondence with the ontology defined in Chapter 2.

This theory-relative character does not constitute a defect in the method. Fairness in the present study does not depend upon criteria that are independent of every theory. It depends upon making the object, conditions, and limits of comparison explicit and applying the same criteria consistently to every comparison case.

N1–N6 are defined prior to their application to individual cases in this manuscript. This ordering does not imply that they were preregistered before the historical research or remained fixed from the beginning of the research process. This study makes no claim that the criteria were preregistered, fixed prior to review, or predetermined before the historical comparison.

The methodology does not evaluate:

- the truth of religious doctrine;
- the philosophical superiority of the compared theory;
- its importance in intellectual history;
- its moral, political, or cultural value;
- direct historical influence upon SIO;
- the truth of SIO itself; or
- the empirical validity of SIO.

Structural similarity does not establish historical influence. Conversely, a determination that a historically prior theory differs structurally from SIO does not diminish that theory’s philosophical significance.

The results of the comparison remain limited to the sources and interpretations examined. Failure to identify structural equivalence in a particular case does not demonstrate the absence of an equivalent ontology from the philosophical record as a whole. Additional primary sources, unexamined traditions, alternative translations, or new interpretations may require revision of an individual assessment or of the overall conclusion.

## 3.9 Comparative Procedure and Summary

Each comparison begins by identifying representative primary sources and relevant scholarship. The compared ontology is then reconstructed in its own terminology and historical context. The analysis distinguishes the lexical, conceptual, structural, ontological, operational, and empirical levels before evaluating N1–N5 individually and N6 as a conjunction condition. For each criterion, the textual evidence, interpretive reconstruction, structural assessment, and relevant qualifications are reported separately.

The purpose of this procedure is not to eliminate interpretive disagreement. It is to make it possible to identify whether a disagreement concerns the selection of sources, the understanding of textual evidence, the reconstruction of the theory, the application of a criterion, or the resulting structural assessment. A reader may dispute an individual item of evidence, interpretation, or assessment without rejecting the comparative method as a whole.

Chapter 2 defined SIO, while the present chapter has defined the rules by which its structure will be compared with other ontologies. The following chapters apply these rules without redefining SIO or modifying the criteria in response to individual cases. Accordingly, every comparison will distinguish explicitly between textual evidence, interpretive reconstruction, and structural assessment.
