1. Introduction

The rapid development of artificial intelligence has brought renewed attention not only to the question of what intelligence is, but also to the more fundamental philosophical question of what a subject is. Advances in large language models, multi-agent systems, social robotics, and social neuroscience increasingly suggest that subjectivity can no longer be adequately understood solely as a property contained within an isolated individual. Across several areas of cognitive science and philosophy, the view that subjects are formed through their interactions with others and with their environments has assumed growing importance.

Despite this shift, however, the ontology underlying these discussions has not necessarily developed at the same pace. Since ancient Greek philosophy, ontology has developed from monism and dualism as central points of departure, followed by a wide range of proposals including process philosophy, phenomenology, nondualism, and relational ontology. These traditions have substantially advanced our understanding of existence, subjectivity, and the world. Nevertheless, it remains possible to interpret many of them as deepening or reconfiguring inherited frameworks when responding to ontology’s fundamental question: by virtue of what does something exist?

Recent developments in cognitive science have introduced a new perspective on this problem. Enactivism, participatory sense-making, social neuroscience, and the psychoanalytic concept of the analytic third have all contributed to a decisive movement toward understanding subjects through relations. These theories have compellingly demonstrated that interaction and co-generation may constitute cognition and experience. Yet although many of them treat relation as a condition through which subjects are formed, they do not necessarily offer an explicit general ontology addressing whether a relation may itself exist as a new subject.

This paper presents Subjectivity Intersection Ontology (SIO) as a conceptual proposal addressing this question. SIO hypothesizes that when differentiated subjects form a constitutive intersection, an irreducible third subjectivity may emerge from that intersection and recursively reconstitute the originating subjects. Importantly, the paper does not present this structure as an empirically established fact. Rather, it formulates SIO as an ontological research program that can be comparatively evaluated and, where its claims extend into formal or empirical domains, exposed to possible falsification.

The study therefore first defines the conceptual structure of SIO and subsequently introduces six evaluative criteria, designated N1–N6, for historical comparison. Using these criteria, it systematically compares SIO with ancient Greek philosophy, ancient and classical Indian philosophy, Christian Trinitarian thought, German Idealism, process philosophy, phenomenology, and contemporary relational ontologies. The purpose is not to assert the superiority of SIO, but to evaluate—through explicit and revisable criteria—whether its structure merely redescribes an existing ontology or may be situated as a distinct ontological option.

The paper also makes a methodological contribution. In addition to explicitly defining the criteria used in comparative ontology, the research was conducted through a public repository in which the development of the manuscript, comparative judgments, review history, and successive revisions were continuously documented. The aim is therefore to make available for examination not only the conclusions of the study but also the process through which those conclusions were formed.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 defines the core concepts of Subjectivity Intersection Ontology. Section 3 presents the six comparative criteria, N1–N6. Sections 4 through 6 conduct the systematic comparison with major historical ontologies and evaluate the resulting similarities and differences. Section 7 considers the implications of SIO for philosophy, cognitive science, artificial intelligence, and neuroscience. The final sections examine the limitations of the present study and identify directions for future research, positioning SIO as a possible conceptual foundation for further interdisciplinary investigation.
