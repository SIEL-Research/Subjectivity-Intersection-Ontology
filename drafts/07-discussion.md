# 7 Discussion

## 7.1 From Substance to Constitutive Intersection

The historical comparison developed in this manuscript suggests an expansion of ontological emphasis from substance, through process and relation, toward the possibility of constitutive intersection. As clarified in Section 6, this sequence is a conceptual organization rather than a chronological timeline. Substance, process, and relation recur across different periods, and many philosophical systems combine more than one of these explanatory forms.

Substance-centered ontologies provide accounts of determinate entities, their properties, and the conditions of their persistence. Process ontologies shift attention toward becoming, development, and event. Relational ontologies examine how entities or subjects depend upon mediation, participation, recognition, or interaction. Each orientation addresses questions that remain relevant, and the transition among them should not be understood as a progressive elimination of earlier positions.

SIO does not reject substance, process, or relation as ontological categories. It proposes a further question within the conceptual space opened by process and relational ontology: can a constitutive relation generate an irreducible subjectivity rather than merely connect, modify, or coordinate subjects that are already constituted? In the structure proposed here, differentiated subjectivities enter an intersection from which a third subjectivity may emerge, and that emergent third may recursively reorganize the originating subjects.

This proposal extends relational ontology only if the third is more than a name for an aggregate, shared description, external observer's construction, or transient pattern of coordination. It would require a defensible account of what makes the emergent term a subjectivity, how it remains irreducible to its participants, and how it can affect their subsequent constitution. The historical review identifies partial precedents for each element but does not establish the validity of their conjunction.

Constitutive intersection should therefore be treated as a candidate ontological principle. Its relation to prior frameworks remains additive and comparative rather than eliminative. Substance may still be needed to explain persistence, process to explain becoming, and relation to explain dependence. Intersection specifies the more restricted possibility that certain relations may become generative of subjectivity.

## 7.2 Implications for Philosophy

The first possible implication concerns subject–object dualism. If subjectivity can be constituted through relations, then a subject cannot always be understood as fully formed prior to its encounter with objects, other subjects, or a shared world. SIO intensifies this claim by asking whether an intersection can acquire a standpoint irreducible to the differentiated standpoints from which it arises. This does not dissolve the distinction between subject and object. It suggests that the distinction may itself require an account of the processes through which subjects and their fields of relevance become determinate.

A second implication concerns the ontological status of relation. In a substance-first model, relations may depend upon relata whose identities are already established. Under a constitutive interpretation, some relations contribute to the identity and capacities of the relata themselves. SIO adds the possibility that a relation may also generate a new center of subjectivity. The proposal consequently requires a distinction among external relation, constitutive relation, emergent organization, and emergent subjectivity. Without that distinction, any coordinated system could be redescribed as a subject, and the framework would lose explanatory specificity.

A third implication concerns emergence. SIO treats the proposed third subjectivity as irreducible to isolated substances or to a simple sum of contributions. Irreducibility, however, can have several meanings. It may concern description, explanation, causal organization, perspective, or ontological status. The present paper adopts an ontological formulation, but further work is needed to show how that claim relates to weaker forms of emergence and what would count against it. The vocabulary of emergence cannot by itself establish that a new subject exists.

A fourth implication concerns recursive subject formation. If an emergent third subjectivity can reorganize the subjects participating in its formation, individuation would be iterative rather than complete at the outset. Subjects would retain differentiation while also acquiring characteristics through the relational structures they sustain. This could provide a conceptual model of how identity, agency, and perspective are formed across multiple levels without reducing the participants to the relation or the relation to the participants.

These implications remain conceptual rather than established conclusions. They also generate unresolved philosophical questions. SIO requires criteria for the identity and persistence of the third subjectivity, an account of its causal or constitutive efficacy, and an explanation of how recursive reconstruction avoids circularity or indefinite regress. It must also specify the boundary conditions under which interaction remains merely interaction and under which it becomes an intersection capable of generating subjectivity.

## 7.3 Implications for Cognitive Science

Cognitive science has increasingly examined cognition as embodied, situated, and interactive. Research on social cognition and enactivism has challenged accounts in which one isolated mind merely represents another from an external standpoint. Participatory approaches instead investigate how coordination and interaction can transform the sense-making of the agents involved and sometimes generate meanings unavailable to either participant independently.

SIO offers a possible ontological interpretation of this interaction-based picture. It asks whether some forms of participatory cognition should be understood not only as coupled individual processes but as the conditions for an emergent subjectivity. Such an interpretation would extend beyond the claim that interaction influences cognition. It would require the interaction to constitute an irreducible standpoint and for that standpoint to participate in the continuing organization of the individual agents.

Collective meaning generation provides a relevant conceptual case. A coordinated encounter can establish shared distinctions, norms, or possibilities for action that were not determined by either participant alone. SIO would interpret these phenomena as evidence of a third subjectivity only if additional criteria were satisfied. Novel meaning, coordination, and interactional autonomy are not individually sufficient to establish subjectivity.

No empirical confirmation follows from this conceptual comparison. Existing findings about social coordination or participatory sense-making would need to be re-examined under explicit operational criteria before they could bear upon SIO. Relevant criteria might include irreducibility to separate-agent models, the persistence of an integrated relational organization, the presence of a distinguishable relational perspective, and reciprocal effects on the participants. These criteria themselves require theoretical clarification and methodological justification.

SIO may therefore function as a hypothesis-generating framework for social cognition rather than as an empirical result. Its usefulness would depend upon whether it supports clearer distinctions, models, and testable contrasts than those already available. If no empirical or explanatory difference can be identified between an emergent third subjectivity and an interaction described without that concept, the ontological interpretation would remain underdetermined.

## 7.4 Implications for Artificial Intelligence

Multi-agent systems provide a controlled domain in which artificial agents coordinate, exchange information, divide tasks, and form collective strategies. Such systems can display behavior not specified at the level of any single agent. Collective intelligence research likewise investigates how distributed interaction can yield problem-solving capacities that exceed those of isolated participants.

SIO may provide a conceptual framework for asking whether emergent organization in interacting intelligent systems could ever amount to emergent subjectivity. It directs attention to the difference between coordinated performance and the formation of a new subject. A multi-agent system may optimize a shared objective or exhibit complex collective behavior without possessing an integrated standpoint of its own.

Applying SIO to artificial systems would therefore require criteria stronger than behavioral novelty. At a conceptual level, one would need to distinguish the individual agents, specify the interaction through which a candidate third is constituted, identify what makes that third irreducible, and show how it reorganizes the participating agents. Further questions concern whether the candidate has a unified perspective, maintains an identity across changing interactions, and exhibits agency that cannot be fully attributed to central control or aggregate computation.

The present framework does not establish that existing or future artificial systems possess subjectivity. Nor does functional coordination alone justify such an attribution. SIO can at most organize the conceptual conditions under which the question might be posed more precisely. Empirical or computational work would then need to determine whether any artificial system satisfies operational counterparts of those conditions.

This distinction is particularly important because terms such as “collective intelligence,” “emergence,” and “agent” can be used without implying phenomenal experience or ontological subjectivity. SIO's possible relevance to artificial intelligence depends upon maintaining these distinctions rather than treating technical agency as sufficient evidence of a subject.

## 7.5 Implications for Neuroscience

This paper does not provide neuroscientific evidence for SIO. It neither identifies a neural mechanism of constitutive intersection nor demonstrates that interacting nervous systems generate an irreducible third subjectivity.

Work on social neuroscience, interpersonal coordination, and inter-brain interaction nevertheless identifies domains in which the conceptual framework proposed here could eventually be examined. These fields investigate how neural, bodily, behavioral, and environmental processes change during interaction. They can therefore provide data relevant to the distinction between parallel individual activity, reciprocal coupling, and relational organization.

Observed synchrony or cross-brain correlation would not by itself establish a third subjectivity. Such patterns may result from common sensory input, shared task structure, coordinated movement, or statistical properties of the measurement. Even interaction-specific neural coupling would demonstrate relational dependence only at the measured level. An ontological claim about emergent subjectivity would require additional arguments connecting those measurements to an integrated standpoint, irreducibility, and recursive efficacy.

Future investigation would require mathematical models that distinguish individual dynamics from interaction-level variables and specify how a candidate relational organization could influence subsequent individual dynamics. Empirical studies would require suitable controls, interventions, temporal resolution, and falsification conditions. They would also need to compare SIO-based models with explanations that retain only coupled individual subjects.

Mathematical or neuroscientific models should not be identified with the ontology itself. They would be operational representations designed to expose consequences, boundary conditions, and possible failures of the proposed structure. A successful model could clarify SIO or generate empirical predictions; it would not, without further argument, prove the existence of an emergent subjectivity.

## 7.6 Toward an Interdisciplinary Research Program

SIO should presently be understood as a research program rather than a completed ontology. The historical analysis identifies a potentially distinct structural conjunction, but it does not resolve the conceptual and evidential questions required to establish that conjunction as an adequate account of reality.

The principal future tasks include:

- **Conceptual refinement.** The framework requires more precise definitions of subjectivity, differentiation, intersection, emergence, irreducibility, and recursive reconstruction. It must also specify identity conditions, levels of analysis, and the boundary between constitutive relation and ordinary interaction.
- **Mathematical formalization.** Formal models could represent the dynamics among originating subjects, an interaction process, and a candidate emergent third. Such models should state their assumptions, invariances, causal structure, and failure conditions without treating a formal variable as equivalent to subjectivity.
- **Historical analysis.** The comparative review should be extended to additional traditions, primary texts, and interpretations. Existing classifications may require revision as the conceptual criteria become more precise.
- **Empirical investigation.** Operational hypotheses could be developed for social cognition, artificial multi-agent systems, and interpersonal neuroscience. Any empirical program would need to distinguish evidence of coordination or emergence from evidence specifically relevant to subjectivity.

Progress across these tasks would require philosophical analysis to remain connected to formal and empirical constraints. Historical comparison can clarify the distinctness of the proposal, mathematical work can specify its structure, and empirical research can test operational consequences. None of these domains can substitute for the others.

The present study therefore represents a starting point rather than an endpoint. Its primary contribution is to establish a conceptual framework through which future philosophical, cognitive-scientific, and empirical work may be systematically organized.
