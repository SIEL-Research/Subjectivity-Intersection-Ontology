## 6.1 Overall Comparison Matrix

The purpose of the following matrix is to consolidate the historical comparisons developed in Chapter 5. The review is intended neither to rank earlier ontologies nor to diminish their philosophical significance. Each tradition addresses problems arising from its own conceptual, historical, and disciplinary context, and the criteria employed here capture only those features relevant to comparison with Subjectivity Intersection Ontology (SIO).

The narrower question is whether any ontology identified in the preceding review explicitly integrates all six criteria—N1 through N6—within a single general ontological framework. ## 6.2 Historical Patterns

At a high level of abstraction, the review reveals a conceptual movement from substance, through process and relation, toward intersection. This sequence does not describe a simple chronological succession. Process-oriented accounts appear in early Greek thought, substance ontologies were developed after them and remained influential, and relational conceptions occur in multiple periods and traditions. The sequence instead organizes the reviewed positions according to increasingly explicit forms of constitutive dependence.

**Substance** designates approaches in which relatively determinate entities, selves, or principles possess ontological priority. Aristotelian substance ontology and Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika realism provide especially clear examples, although their categories and historical contexts differ. Such frameworks can recognize genuine relations while treating them as dependent upon the entities related.

**Process** shifts explanatory priority toward becoming, development, event, or self-articulation. Heraclitus, Schelling, Hegel, and Whitehead exemplify different forms of this shift. They do not share a single theory of process: ordered change, natural productivity, dialectical development, and concrescence are distinct structures. What unites them for the present comparison is the claim that actuality cannot be understood adequately as a static inventory of substances.

**Relation** identifies frameworks in which entities or subjects are partly constituted through dependence, mediation, recognition, participation, or intercorporeality. Trinitarian metaphysics, Buddhist dependent origination, Hegelian recognition, Nishida's logic of place, Merleau-Ponty's intercorporeality, and contemporary enactive theories all assign constitutive significance to relation, but they do so at different explanatory levels.

**Intersection**, as used in this manuscript, specifies a narrower structure. It concerns differentiated subjectivities whose constitutive encounter may generate a third subjectivity irreducible to either participant and capable of recursively reconstructing them. Participatory Sense-Making and the Analytic Third come closest to parts of this structure at the level of social cognition and psychoanalytic practice, respectively. Neither, as reviewed here, presents the entire structure as a general ontology.

The conceptual sequence can therefore be represented as:

Substance  
↓  
Process  
↓  
Relation  
↓  
Intersection

This ordering should not be interpreted as the replacement of earlier ontologies by later ones. Substance, process, and relation answer different explanatory questions and frequently coexist within the same philosophical system. Each stage extends the available conceptual vocabulary for describing existence. SIO proposes constitutive intersection as an additional ontological principle within that expanded vocabulary. The historical review does not establish whether this proposal is ultimately adequate.

## 6.3 Historical Gaps

The comparison identifies substantial achievements across the traditions reviewed. Ancient Greek philosophy established several principal alternatives that shaped Western ontology, including monism, process, form-based metaphysics, substance ontology, and emanation. Ancient Indian philosophy developed highly differentiated accounts of consciousness, selfhood, nonduality, dualism, substance, dependent origination, emptiness, pluralism, and recognition. These traditions cannot be reduced to Western categories or to a single Indian position.

Trinitarian ontology developed one of the most extensive relational metaphysics in the Western tradition by integrating unity, hypostatic distinction, eternal relation, and mutual indwelling. Its theological domain and its account of eternal relations distinguish it from a general theory of emergent subjectivity. German Idealism advanced developmental ontology through nature's productivity, mediation, negation, recognition, and the historical forms of Spirit, while Schelling and Hegel offered significantly different accounts of systematic development.

Twentieth-century process philosophy and phenomenology reconceived subjectivity dynamically. Whitehead treated actual occasions as relational processes of becoming; Nishida situated self and world within the self-determination of place; and Merleau-Ponty analyzed embodied subjectivity through perception, intercorporeality, and Flesh. Contemporary theories moved further toward constitutive accounts of interaction. Participatory Sense-Making attributes a limited autonomy to organized interaction, and the Analytic Third describes an intersubjectively generated field that shapes both clinical participants. Cosmopsychism, from a different direction, treats subjectivity at the level of the cosmos as fundamental.

These achievements progressively clarify individual elements relevant to SIO. Some traditions establish ontological subjectivity without constitutive intersection. Others develop constitutive relation without an emergent subject. Still others describe an emergent relational third within a restricted practical domain without presenting it as an ontologically real subjectivity of general scope. Several incorporate feedback or transformation but do not connect it to the emergence of an irreducible third subject.

Within the scope of the historical review conducted in this paper, no single previously reviewed ontology has been found to integrate simultaneously:

- differentiated subjectivities;
- constitutive intersection;
- an irreducible emergent third subjectivity; and
- recursive reconstruction of the originating subjectivities

as one general ontological framework. This conclusion is limited in three respects. First, it concerns only the traditions and representative formulations examined in Chapter 5. Second, the individual assessments depend upon contestable interpretations of extensive and internally diverse literatures. Third, failure to identify a formulation is not proof that no such formulation exists elsewhere in the historical record.

The identified gap is therefore a result of the present comparative review, not a claim of exhaustive historical absence. Further work may alter particular grades, identify additional precedents, or show that a previously reviewed theory satisfies a criterion more fully than the current analysis recognizes.

## 6.4 Overall Assessment

The purpose of this paper is not to establish the truth of SIO. Historical distinctness, even if supported by further research, would not demonstrate ontological adequacy. Nor does the absence of an identified equivalent within the present corpus establish that SIO is unprecedented in all philosophical literature.

The contribution of the manuscript is more limited. It defines SIO through explicit structural criteria, compares those criteria systematically with major ontological alternatives, and uses a common framework to assess potential historical novelty. This method distinguishes verbal or thematic resemblance from complete structural equivalence and allows individual evaluations to be revised without abandoning the entire comparison.

The review indicates that the components associated with SIO have substantial precedents. Ontologically fundamental subjectivity, constitutive relation, emergent organization, triadic structure, and recursive transformation have each been developed in significant philosophical, theological, psychoanalytic, and cognitive-scientific contexts. Their distribution across the matrix explains why several traditions display important proximity while none receives an S grade.

Based on the review presented here, Subjectivity Intersection Ontology may represent a distinct ontological research program that combines structural elements previously developed separately across multiple philosophical traditions. This conclusion remains provisional. Its strength depends upon the adequacy of the N1–N6 definitions, the interpretations advanced in Chapter 5, the representativeness of the traditions selected, and future comparison with sources not yet examined.
