# 1. Introduction

The rapid development of artificial intelligence has brought renewed attention not only to the question of what intelligence is, but also to the more fundamental question of what a subject is. Large language models, social AI, multi-agent systems, and advances in neuroscience have intensified debate about subjectivity and interaction. Yet the ontology underlying these debates has not necessarily developed at the same pace.

What is existence? For more than two millennia, Western philosophy has largely developed from monism and dualism as basic points of departure. Phenomenology, process philosophy, relational ontology, and non-dualism have since introduced important alternatives and reinterpretations, but many of these approaches deepen or transform inherited frameworks rather than substantially expanding the basic set of ontological possibilities.

At the same time, contemporary cognitive science and social neuroscience increasingly recognize that subjects are formed within relations with others. Enactivism, participatory sense-making, and the analytic third have offered rich accounts of the role of relation in cognition, meaning-making, and transformation. Even so, relation is generally treated as a condition that shapes or modifies subjects, rather than as something that may itself exist as a subject.

This paper addresses that gap by proposing **Subjectivity Intersection Ontology (SIO)**. SIO advances the hypothesis that when two differentiated subjectivities enter into a constitutive relation, an irreducible third subjectivity may emerge from their intersection, and that this third subjectivity may in turn recursively reconstitute the originating subjectivities. The distinctive claim is therefore not merely that relations matter, but that under specified conditions relation may become an ontological source of subjectivity itself.

This paper does not claim to establish the truth of that hypothesis. Its first aim is to define SIO conceptually and clarify its historical position. To do so, it introduces six evaluative criteria (N1–N6) and compares SIO with major ontological and relational traditions from Ancient Greek philosophy through theology, modern and contemporary philosophy, psychoanalysis, cognitive science, and cosmopsychism. The central question is whether SIO is simply a reformulation of existing theories or whether it presents a genuinely distinct ontological option.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 defines the core concepts of SIO. Section 3 introduces the six evaluative criteria. Section 4 reviews major historical precedents and contemporary analogues. Section 5 compares these traditions under the N1–N6 framework and assesses the provisional novelty claim. The final sections discuss limitations, open problems, and future directions for philosophical, mathematical, and empirical investigation.
