1. Introduction

The rapid development of artificial intelligence has brought renewed attention not only to the question of what intelligence is, but also to the more fundamental philosophical question of what subjectivity is.

Developments in large language models, multi-agent systems, social robotics, and social neuroscience have brought into view the limits of understanding intelligence and cognition solely as properties contained within isolated individuals. Across several areas of cognitive science and philosophy of mind, the view that subjects are formed through interaction with others and with their environments has assumed increasing importance.

The ontological problem raised by this relational turn, however, has not necessarily been made fully explicit. The claim that interaction may constitute cognition, meaning, or experience is not identical to the claim that a new subjectivity may be constituted through that interaction. The former may explain how existing subjects are transformed through relation. The latter asks whether relation or intersection itself may constitute a further subjectivity irreducible to any of the subjects from which it arises. This second question extends beyond problems of social cognition and intersubjectivity to an ontological problem: in virtue of what, and through what structure, does something exist as a single existent?

Since Descartes, an influential strand of modern philosophical discussion of subjectivity has treated the certainty of the individual thinking subject as an important point of departure. The cogito, expressed in the proposition “I think, therefore I am,” established subjectivity as a foundational problem of modern philosophy. Subsequent traditions developed this problem in multiple directions through intersubjectivity, embodiment, process, relation, and emergence. This paper does not deny the importance of asking how an individual subject relates to others. The question addressed here, however, is not limited to relations among human individuals. More generally, it asks whether the intersection of differentiated subjectivities may itself constitute a further irreducible subjectivity. This is not a straightforward extension of modern theories that begin from individual consciousness, but an attempt to reconsider the ontological constitution of subjectivity as such.

Western ontology has often taken monism and dualism as central points of departure while developing diverse accounts of substance, process, generation, relation, and nonduality. Ancient and classical Indian philosophy, Christian Trinitarian ontology, German Idealism, process philosophy, phenomenology, psychoanalysis, and contemporary cognitive science have likewise proposed sophisticated theories concerning subjectivity, difference, relation, third terms, and generation. These traditions have provided important alternatives to accounts of existence as a collection of static and isolated substances. The recognition of triadicity, relationality, or emergence, however, does not by itself establish an ontology in which a third subjectivity is constituted through intersection. Similarity of vocabulary must therefore be distinguished from equivalence of ontological structure.

This paper presents Subjectivity Intersection Ontology (SIO) as a conceptual proposal addressing this problem. SIO is presented neither as a reinterpretation of an existing ontology nor as an empirically established theory. It is first defined as an explicit ontological proposal, with its conceptual consequences subsequently opened to formalization and empirical examination.

SIO defines subjectivity as perspective. In its ontological prototype, Relative Subjectivity is differentiated through the projection of Absolute Subjectivity, and an Intersection Subjectivity irreducible to either emerges in the interval between them. The canonical operational form through which this prototype is extended to multiple Relative Subjectivities is:

\[
AB \rightarrow C \rightarrow AB.
\]

Here, A and B are differentiated Relative Subjectivities, while C is the third subjectivity constituted through their constitutive intersection. C is defined not as the sum or average of A and B, shared content, or an external observer’s description, but as a subjectivity irreducible to either. C further acts back upon A and B and reconstitutes their modes of existence. This canonical operational form does not replace the ontological prototype consisting of Absolute Subjectivity, Relative Subjectivity, and Intersection Subjectivity. The relation between the prototype and its operational form is distinguished rigorously in Chapter 2.

This paper addresses three central questions. First, how can SIO be defined in terms of its own ontological structure without being reduced to an established philosophical vocabulary? Second, how can SIO be compared with historical ontologies without conflating vocabulary or conceptual similarity with structural equivalence? Third, within the comparative set examined here, does any theory clearly integrate the following within a single ontology: differentiated subjectivities, constitutive intersection, an irreducible third subjectivity, the reconstitutive action of that subjectivity upon the subjectivities from which it arose, and the general theoretical scope of this structure?

To address these questions, the paper derives six comparative criteria, N1–N6, from the structure of SIO. N1–N5 independently assess the subjective status of the candidate third term, the direction of generation through intersection, the reconstitution of the originating subjectivities, the ontological status of the candidate third term, and the generality and theoretical scope of the structure. N6 is the conjunction condition assessing whether these criteria are integrated within a single general ontology. The criteria are not a scale for scoring the philosophical value, historical importance, or truth of the theories under comparison. They constitute an SIO-relative framework for assessing criterion-specific structural correspondence with the ontology defined in this paper.

The methodology is applied to twenty-one comparison targets selected from ancient Greek philosophy, ancient and classical Indian philosophy, Christian Trinitarian ontology, German Idealism, process ontology, phenomenology, psychoanalysis, cognitive science, and contemporary philosophy of mind. For each target, the analysis distinguishes textual evidence, interpretive reconstruction within the theory’s own terms, and structural assessment under N1–N6. This separation allows disagreements concerning the selection of texts, historical interpretation, and the application of comparative criteria to be examined as problems arising at distinct analytical levels.

The comparison identifies individual structures relevant to SIO in different forms across multiple traditions. Third terms, relational constitution, generation, the transformation of subjectivity, and general ontological scope are distributed across the comparative set rather than concentrated within any single theory. Kashmir Śaivism, Schelling, Hegel, and Nishida exhibit partial or interpretation-dependent satisfaction of N6. None of the twenty-one targets examined, however, clearly satisfied N1–N5 and integrated them within a single general ontology in a manner that clearly satisfied N6.

This result does not establish the absolute novelty of SIO or its chronological priority in the history of thought. Nor does it demonstrate the truth of SIO, its superiority to other ontologies, or the empirical reality of third subjectivity. The conclusion warranted by this study is restricted to the specified comparative set, evaluative criteria, and interpretations of the literature. Within those limits, SIO cannot be identified as a merely terminological restatement of any one of the theories examined. The paper therefore positions SIO not as a completed metaphysical system, but as an unfinished ontological research program whose conceptual coherence, formal precision, and empirical discriminability remain to be examined.

In addition to its conceptual contribution, this paper has two methodological aims. The first is to make explicit the criteria that often remain implicit in comparative ontology and to distinguish the vocabulary, conceptual, structural, ontological, operational, and empirical levels of comparison. The second is to document the development of the manuscript, comparative assessments, and revision history in a public repository, thereby making available for examination not only the conclusions of the study but also the process through which they were formed. This public record is documentation of the research process, not evidence supporting the conclusions of the paper. Public discussion likewise does not replace formal peer review, but provides a complementary record of the manuscript’s development.

The paper aims to present its ontological proposal, comparative method, historical comparison, philosophical examination, and research methodology as a single transparent research program.

The paper proceeds as follows. Chapter 2 defines SIO in its own terms and distinguishes its ontological prototype from its canonical operational form. Chapter 3 establishes the levels of comparison, N1–N6, and the assessment procedure. Chapter 4 applies this methodology to twenty-one comparison targets and presents textual evidence, interpretive reconstruction, and structural assessment for each case. Chapter 5 integrates the assessments from Chapter 4 without modifying them and formulates the absence of clear structural equivalence within the comparative set as a delimited proposition. Chapter 6 examines the philosophical implications of SIO for subjectivity and objectivity, intersubjectivity, relation, emergence, individuation, temporality, epistemology, and ethics, and develops a research program encompassing formalization, operationalization, empirical discriminability, and conditions of failure. Chapter 7 clarifies the achievements of the study, the limits of its claims, and the final position of SIO. Taken together, these chapters aim to position SIO as an ontological research program that is conceptually explicit, methodologically transparent, and open to empirical examination.
