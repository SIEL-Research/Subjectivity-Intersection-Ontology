4. Historical Comparison of Ontologies

## 4.1 Purpose and Scope of This Chapter

This chapter applies the comparative method established in Chapter 3 to major historical and contemporary ontologies. Its purpose is not to rank the philosophical value of these theories, but to examine their structural correspondence with Subjectivity Intersection Ontology (SIO) individually under N1–N6.

The cases were included for two reasons. First, they present representative structures concerning unity, plurality, substance, process, emanation, nonduality, or dependent constitution that have exerted a continuing influence on subsequent ontology. Second, their accounts of triadicity, constitutive relation, the ontological status of subjectivity, or the emergence of relational organization permit direct comparison with at least one of N1–N6. Not every case must satisfy both considerations to the same degree, but each has a clearly identifiable comparative role under at least one of them.

On this basis, the chapter examines six lineages: ancient Greek philosophy; Indian philosophy; Christian Trinitarian ontology; German Idealism; process ontology and phenomenology; and contemporary relational theories.

The selection is not exhaustive of the world's ontological traditions. Traditions not examined here, including Chinese philosophy, Islamic philosophy, Jewish philosophy, and mystical thought, may contain further candidates requiring comparison. A failure to identify structural equivalence in this chapter would therefore not establish that no equivalent structure exists anywhere in the history of philosophy.

Each comparison distinguishes textual evidence, interpretive reconstruction, and structural assessment. The textual-evidence subsection identifies representative primary sources and their principal concepts. The interpretive-reconstruction subsection describes how those concepts relate within the theory itself. The structural-assessment subsection then evaluates that reconstruction under N1–N6.

The assessments used in this chapter are limited to four categories:

- clear satisfaction;
- partial or interpretation-dependent satisfaction;
- non-satisfaction; and
- insufficient evidence.

An assessment under N4 concerns the ontological status assigned to the candidate third term within the compared theory. It does not imply that this term is identical to Intersection Subjectivity in SIO. Whether the third term is a subjectivity is assessed independently under N1, and whether it is generated through intersection is assessed independently under N2.

N6 is not an additional scoring item appended to N1–N5. It is a conjunction condition assessing whether those criteria are integrated within a single general ontology. N6 receives clear satisfaction only when N1–N5 are all clearly satisfied and integrated within one ontology. If any of N1–N5 is not satisfied, N6 is also not satisfied. If none is judged not satisfied, but at least one criterion or their integration remains interpretation-dependent, N6 receives partial or interpretation-dependent satisfaction.

These assessments are neither added together nor converted into an overall grade. Nor does this chapter classify individual theories by summary labels such as “precedent” or “closest comparison.” Cross-case evaluation is reserved for Chapter 5.

## 4.2 Ancient Greek Philosophy

Ancient Greek philosophy established major ontological alternatives concerning unity and plurality, being and becoming, form and appearance, substance and relation, and origin and derivation. This section examines Parmenides, Heraclitus, Plato, Aristotle, and Plotinus.

### 4.2.1 Parmenides

#### Textual Evidence

The “Way of Truth” in Parmenides' poem presents what-is as ungenerated, imperishable, complete, indivisible, and unchanging. What-is cannot arise from what-is-not or pass into what-is-not. Becoming, destruction, motion, and plurality do not belong to being in the strict sense, but are assigned to the domain of mortal opinion.

#### Interpretive Reconstruction

Within this structure, the unity and self-identity of being have ontological priority. Difference and becoming are not admitted as constitutive of being itself. The structural conditions required for a new subjectivity to arise from a relation between differentiated subjectivities are therefore absent.

Like SIO, Parmenides addresses primordial unity. That unity, however, is not presented as internally containing perspectival difference or intersection. A shared concern with unity at the level of problem formulation does not entail structural correspondence.

#### Structural Assessment

N1, N2, and N3 are not satisfied. No third subjectivity, generation of such a subjectivity through intersection, or action upon originating subjectivities is proposed. N4 is also not satisfied: although being in general is assigned a strong reality, there is no candidate third term. N5 and N6 are not satisfied because the structure corresponding to SIO is not proposed.

**Limitation of the present comparison:** Parmenides' work survives only in fragments, and the retrospective application of modern concepts of subjectivity to his ontology is necessarily limited.

### 4.2.2 Heraclitus

#### Textual Evidence

The fragments of Heraclitus depict the cosmos as ordered through change, tension, opposition, and transformation. Opposites are not merely exclusive, but are mutually related within a single order. *Logos* appears as the principle indicating that such change follows a common order rather than constituting arbitrary flux.

#### Interpretive Reconstruction

In Heraclitus, stability may be understood not as the negation of change but as an order maintained through change. Opposed terms form a structure through their tension or interdependence. In this respect, the account holds difference and unity together.

The opposed terms, however, are not defined as differentiated subjectivities, nor does their relation generate an irreducible third subjectivity. The *logos* is likewise not a third subjectivity constituted through the intersection of two subjectivities.

#### Structural Assessment

Process, opposition, and relation provide points of conceptual comparison, but the structures specified by N1–N3 are not identifiable. N4 is not satisfied because no candidate third term is assigned subjective and ontological status. Although Heraclitus' cosmology has general scope, it does not present the SIO-type structure addressed by N5 as a general principle; N5 is therefore not satisfied. N6 is also not satisfied.

**Limitation of the present comparison:** The assessment depends on a fragmentary textual tradition and on the ordering and interpretation of those fragments. It does not resolve the interpretive disputes concerning the *logos* and the unity of opposites.

### 4.2.3 Plato

#### Textual Evidence

Plato's dialogues discuss in different ways the distinction between sensible particulars and intelligible Forms, the participation of particulars in Forms, and the relations among Forms. The *Phaedo* and the *Republic* emphasize the stability of intelligible Forms, while the *Parmenides* and the *Sophist* examine participation, difference, being, and the relations among kinds in more complex terms.

#### Interpretive Reconstruction

In Platonic ontology, the determinacy of particulars is explained through their relation to Forms. In the later dialogues, it becomes difficult to understand being simply as an inventory of isolated Forms, and difference and relation enter into ontological analysis.

Forms, particulars, and participation, however, do not constitute a structure of two differentiated subjectivities and a third subjectivity arising from their intersection. Dialectical mediation and relations among Forms do not themselves become new subjectivities.

#### Structural Assessment

N1–N3 are not satisfied. N4 is not satisfied because there is no candidate third subjectivity. Although the theory of Forms is presented as a general ontology, it does not present an SIO-type generative structure as a general principle; N5 is therefore not satisfied. N6 is also not satisfied.

**Limitation of the present comparison:** Plato's ontology develops through dialogues with differing problems and argumentative trajectories. Any reconstruction of the theory of Forms, participation, and relations among kinds as a single fixed system is therefore limited.

### 4.2.4 Aristotle

#### Textual Evidence

In the *Categories*, primary substances are treated as individual beings that are neither predicated of a subject nor present in a subject. Relation is recognized as one category, but ordinarily depends upon the relata. In the *Metaphysics*, substance is analyzed through form, matter, and their composite, with form playing a central role in explaining what a thing is.

#### Interpretive Reconstruction

Aristotle does not deny the reality or explanatory importance of relations. Ontological priority is nevertheless assigned principally to substance. Relations may connect, qualify, and explain substances, but relation itself is not treated as an ontological source that generates a new subjectivity.

This structure contrasts with the SIO proposal that relation may not merely connect existing terms but may constitute a third subjectivity. The contrast does not imply that Aristotle regarded relations as unreal.

#### Structural Assessment

N1–N4 are not satisfied. Aristotle's theories of substance and relation have general scope, but they do not present the generation of a third subjectivity through intersection as a general principle; N5 is therefore not satisfied. N6 is consequently not satisfied.

**Limitation of the present comparison:** This assessment is based principally on the accounts of substance and relation in the *Categories* and the *Metaphysics*. It does not reduce Aristotle's philosophy as a whole—including his accounts of soul, community, and generation—to a simple “substantialism.”

### 4.2.5 Plotinus

#### Textual Evidence

The *Enneads* present a hierarchical structure of the One, Intellect (*Nous*), and Soul. The One exceeds being and plurality. Intellect proceeds from the One and contains the intelligible Forms as a unified multiplicity, while Soul proceeds from Intellect and mediates between intelligible order and the sensible cosmos. Procession is accompanied by return toward the source.

#### Interpretive Reconstruction

Plotinus' structure is triadic, and Intellect and Soul are assigned derivative yet real status. Procession and return describe more than a static hierarchy: what proceeds remains directed toward its source.

Intellect, however, is not generated through the intersection of two differentiated subjectivities, and Soul is not a third subjectivity constituted through the intersection of two prior subjectivities. The direction of constitution is asymmetrical, proceeding from source to derivative terms; the derived Soul does not reconstitute the One and Intellect.

#### Structural Assessment

The triadic structure and the subjective character attributable to Intellect and Soul provide partial and interpretation-dependent correspondence under N1. N2 and N3 are not satisfied.

The ontological reality of Intellect and Soul is itself clear. It is nevertheless interpretation-dependent which term in the hierarchy of the One, Intellect, and Soul should be selected as the candidate third term for comparison with SIO. N4 therefore receives partial or interpretation-dependent rather than clear satisfaction. Because the triadic cosmology is presented as a general ontology, N5 is clearly satisfied. Since N2 and N3 are not satisfied, N6 is not satisfied.

**Limitation of the present comparison:** Applying the modern comparative terms “subjectivity” and “third term” to the One, Intellect, and Soul is itself an interpretive reconstruction whose correspondence is not guaranteed by identical terminology in the primary texts.

## 4.3 Indian Philosophy

Indian philosophy developed diverse and mutually opposed ontologies of consciousness, self, substance, causation, dependence, plurality, change, and nonduality. This section examines separately the Upaniṣads and Advaita Vedānta, Sāṃkhya and Classical Yoga, Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika, early Buddhism, Madhyamaka, Jain philosophy, and Kashmir Śaivism.

These traditions have distinct historical contexts, primary texts, problems, and conceptual systems. “Indian philosophy” cannot therefore be treated as a single unified ontology. Each case is subject to the same evidential burden: representative texts must be identified, the theory must be reconstructed internally, and N1–N6 must be assessed individually.

### 4.3.1 The Upaniṣads and Advaita Vedānta

#### Textual Evidence

The early Upaniṣads discuss the relation between self (*ātman*) and ultimate reality (*brahman*) in diverse forms. In the later system of Advaita Vedānta, particularly as associated with Śaṅkara, the true self is understood not as body, psyche, or personal history, but as pure consciousness non-different from *brahman*.

The Upaniṣads are not a single systematic work and contain multiple formulations concerning self, cosmos, unity, and difference. The present reconstruction does not reduce all of them to one doctrine, but concentrates on the nondual interpretation systematized in Advaita.

#### Interpretive Reconstruction

Empirical plurality operates at the level of conventional experience, but at the level of ultimate reality consciousness is not divided into numerically independent selves. Liberation occurs not through the generation of a new subjectivity from the intersection of multiple subjectivities, but through recognition of the non-difference of *ātman* and *brahman*.

#### Structural Assessment

N1–N3 are not satisfied because there is no intersection of differentiated subjectivities and no generation of a third subjectivity. Although consciousness is assigned ultimate reality, N4 is not satisfied because no candidate third term is proposed. Since no SIO-type structure is formulated as a general principle, N5 and N6 are also not satisfied.

**Limitation of the present comparison:** Combining the plurality of positions in the Upaniṣads with the systematic Advaita of the later Śaṅkara tradition in a single entry has clear limits. The assessment does not presuppose their complete identity.

### 4.3.2 Sāṃkhya and Classical Yoga

#### Textual Evidence

Sāṃkhya, as represented by the *Sāṃkhyakārikā*, proposes a dualism of *puruṣa*, pure consciousness, and *prakṛti*, changing primordial nature. From *prakṛti* develop intellect, ego, mind, sensory capacities, subtle elements, and the material world. Classical Yoga adopts much of this ontology and connects liberation to discrimination between *puruṣa* and *prakṛti*.

Classical Sāṃkhya generally recognizes a plurality of *puruṣas*, each of which is immutable and witnesses the changes of *prakṛti*. The recognition in Classical Yoga of Īśvara as a special *puruṣa* does not dissolve the fundamental distinction between consciousness and nature.

#### Interpretive Reconstruction

Cosmological evolution begins through the proximity of *puruṣa* and *prakṛti*, but *puruṣa* neither changes nor acts. The generated principles are transformations of *prakṛti*. Their conjunction makes experience possible, but does not constitute a third subjectivity from their intersection.

#### Structural Assessment

The difference and conjunction of two principles are recognized, but no third subjectivity is generated. N1–N4 are therefore not satisfied. Although Sāṃkhya presents a general cosmology, it does not generalize an SIO-type structure of intersection; N5 and N6 are not satisfied.

**Limitation of the present comparison:** Sāṃkhya and Classical Yoga differ concerning Īśvara, practice, and liberation. The assessment is restricted to the *puruṣa–prakṛti* structure they share.

### 4.3.3 Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika

#### Textual Evidence

Nyāya and Vaiśeṣika developed systems of categories including substance, quality, motion, universal, particularity, inherence, and, in later formulations, absence. The self is treated as an enduring substance, while material reality includes atoms and composites. Relations such as conjunction, disjunction, and inherence explain composites, qualities, and part–whole structures.

The two schools did not originate as a single system, but became integrated through historical development. Their number of categories, accounts of self, and relation to epistemology vary across periods. The present comparison considers their mature realist categorial framework.

#### Interpretive Reconstruction

Within this framework, relations play an important role in organizing reality and explaining the constitution of composites. Subjectivity, however, belongs primarily to enduring selves and is not newly generated through relation. Even where a composite whole is not reducible to a mere aggregate of parts, the emergence of a new subjectivity does not follow.

#### Structural Assessment

The reality of relations and wholes is recognized, but the generation of a third subjectivity and reconstitution of originating subjectivities are not proposed. N1–N4 are therefore not satisfied. Nor is an SIO-type structure generalized; N5 and N6 are not satisfied.

**Limitation of the present comparison:** This assessment reconstructs the schools' long historical development in terms of a mature categorial realism and does not evaluate every ontological and epistemological difference between periods.

### 4.3.4 Early Buddhism

#### Textual Evidence

Early Buddhist texts analyze the person in terms of changing components such as the five aggregates and maintain that no permanent, autonomous self can be found. Dependent origination explains phenomena as arising in dependence upon conditions, accounting for experience, action, suffering, and continuity without positing an enduring self-substance.

These doctrines were developed into different ontological and epistemological systems by later Buddhist schools. This section therefore treats early Buddhism not as a completed and unified relational ontology, but as a historical structure defined by non-self and conditioned arising.

#### Interpretive Reconstruction

This structure permits comparison with constitutive relation insofar as it prioritizes conditioned arising over independent substance. Its analytical direction, however, is not to generate a third subjectivity from the intersection of multiple subjectivities, but to dismantle attachment to an enduring subjective substance.

Dependent origination has partial conceptual correspondence with generation through intersection, but what arises is not defined as a third subjectivity constituted between two subjectivities.

#### Structural Assessment

N1 and N3 are not satisfied. N2 receives partial correspondence because of the structure of conditioned arising, but the generation does not occur through an intersection between differentiated subjectivities. N4 is not satisfied because no candidate third term is assigned ontological reality. Although dependent origination has broad scope, it does not generalize an SIO-type structure; N5 therefore receives only partial satisfaction. Since N1, N3, and N4 are not satisfied, N6 is not satisfied.

**Limitation of the present comparison:** “Early Buddhism” encompasses multiple textual strata and doctrines. “Relational ontology” is also a modern interpretive term rather than the tradition's own description.

### 4.3.5 Madhyamaka

#### Textual Evidence

Madhyamaka, originating with Nāgārjuna's *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*, argues that all phenomena lack intrinsic nature (*svabhāva*). Emptiness does not signify simple nonexistence; it indicates that phenomena lack independent self-grounding and function in dependence upon causal, conceptual, and practical conditions. Emptiness itself is not presented as a new substance.

The distinction between conventional and ultimate truth prevents the critique of intrinsic reality from becoming a wholesale denial of ordinary or practical causation. Because Madhyamaka itself contains interpretive differences, this section does not reduce all of its forms to a single doctrine.

#### Interpretive Reconstruction

Madhyamaka offers a powerful critique of treating independent substances as the ultimate units of ontology. Phenomena are constituted through relation and dependence, but those dependencies are not said to generate a new subjective substance. Indeed, assigning intrinsic reality to a third term arising from intersection could be criticized as a reintroduction of *svabhāva*.

#### Structural Assessment

N1 and N3 are not satisfied. The general structure of dependent constitution provides partial correspondence under N2. N4 is not satisfied because no candidate third term is assigned independent ontological status. Although the analysis of dependence has general scope, it does not generalize the SIO structure of a third subjectivity; N5 receives partial satisfaction. Since N1, N3, and N4 are not satisfied, N6 is not satisfied.

**Limitation of the present comparison:** Madhyamaka contains major interpretive differences concerning the two truths, emptiness, language, and the status of ontological claims. This assessment does not fix those differences into a single metaphysical position.

### 4.3.6 Jain Philosophy

#### Textual Evidence

Jain philosophy presents a pluralist realism recognizing many individual living selves (*jīva*) and non-living realities (*ajīva*). Substances persist while undergoing modal change. The doctrine of many-sidedness (*anekāntavāda*) holds that reality cannot be exhausted by a single unqualified proposition and requires qualified descriptions from different standpoints.

Conditional predication does not reduce truth to arbitrary subjectivity. It specifies the standpoint and qualification under which a proposition is valid, thereby addressing both the many-sided character of reality and the partial character of cognition.

#### Interpretive Reconstruction

In Jain philosophy, perspectival plurality enables different aspects of the same reality to be described appropriately. Multiple standpoints are not reduced to arbitrary subjectivism, but neither does their intersection generate a new third subjectivity.

#### Structural Assessment

Multiple subjects and perspectives are recognized, but no irreducible third subjectivity is proposed; N1 is not satisfied. Although there is a limited resemblance in the conditioning of standpoints, this does not constitute the generation of subjectivity through intersection, so N2 is not satisfied. N3 and N4 are also not satisfied. Perspectival plurality is presented as a general principle, but not as an SIO-type structure; N5 receives partial satisfaction. N6 is not satisfied.

**Limitation of the present comparison:** *Anekāntavāda* spans ontology, logic, epistemology, and theories of predication. Reading it as a theory of intersection between subjectivities is therefore conceptually limited.

### 4.3.7 Kashmir Śaivism

#### Textual Evidence

Kashmir Śaivism, particularly the Pratyabhijñā tradition associated with Utpaladeva and Abhinavagupta, understands ultimate reality as Śiva. Śiva is not a static One, but self-luminous consciousness inseparable from Śakti, the power of differentiation, manifestation, and action. The world is a real manifestation of consciousness, while finite subjects arise through the contraction or limitation of universal consciousness.

Recognition (*pratyabhijñā*) means that the finite subject recognizes its ultimate identity as Śiva. Triadic structures such as knower, knowing, and known are also discussed within nondual consciousness.

“Kashmir Śaivism” does not designate a single homogeneous school. The present comparison primarily addresses the systematic ontology of consciousness developed in the Pratyabhijñā tradition while preserving its differences from other Śaiva traditions.

#### Interpretive Reconstruction

This ontology integrates unitary consciousness, differentiated manifestation, triadic structures, and transformation of finite subjectivity. The finite subject changes not through the external addition of a new entity, but through recognition of its already-established identity with universal consciousness.

Its direction of constitution therefore differs from SIO, in which a third subjectivity is generated through the intersection of differentiated subjectivities. Triadicity arises within Śiva's self-manifestation; a third subjectivity is not explicitly said to arise anew between two subjectivities.

#### Structural Assessment

The triadic structure of consciousness provides partial and interpretation-dependent correspondence under N1. Because differentiation and manifestation are understood dynamically, N2 also receives partial correspondence, although this is not identical to generation through intersection. Recognition transforms finite subjectivity, providing partial correspondence under N3.

It is clear that consciousness and its manifestations are assigned ontological reality. It is not unambiguous, however, which among the several triadic structures—knower, knowing, and known; Śiva, Śakti, and manifestation; or others—should be selected as the candidate corresponding to SIO's third subjectivity. N4 therefore receives partial or interpretation-dependent satisfaction at this stage.

The structure is presented as a general ontology, so N5 is clearly satisfied. Although none of N1–N4 is judged not satisfied, their satisfaction and integration remain interpretation-dependent; N6 therefore receives partial or interpretation-dependent satisfaction.

**Limitation of the present comparison:** This assessment is based principally on the systematic account of the Pratyabhijñā school. The extraction of a candidate corresponding to SIO from the several triadic structures within Kashmir Śaivism is itself an interpretive choice.

## 4.4 Trinitarian Ontology and Christian Metaphysics

### 4.4.1 Textual Evidence

The Council of Nicaea in 325 expressed, through the term *homoousios*, that the Son is of the same essence or substance as the Father. The Council of Constantinople in 381 reaffirmed this direction and provided a more explicit formulation of the status of the Holy Spirit.

The Cappadocian Fathers played an important role in establishing the terminology that distinguishes one essence (*ousia*) from three hypostases (*hypostaseis*). Augustine's *De Trinitate* analyzed their distinction through relation while maintaining the equality and unity of the three. Later Eastern and Western traditions addressed the eternal relations of origin among Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, mutual indwelling (*perichoresis*), and hypostases distinguished by relation.

“Trinitarian ontology” was not expressed through a single, identical vocabulary and theory in every period and confession. Eastern and Western traditions developed different emphases and formulations concerning hypostasis, essence, relation, and the procession of the Holy Spirit. The present section acknowledges these differences while examining the basic structure shared within the Nicene tradition.

### 4.4.2 Interpretive Reconstruction

The basic Trinitarian structure maintains simultaneously one divine essence and three distinct hypostases: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. The three are neither parts composing a whole nor three independent substances belonging to the same kind. Relation is not an external addition to already completed individuals, but an internal element in understanding hypostatic distinction.

*Hypostasis*, however, cannot be identified without qualification with modern individual consciousness or with subjectivity in SIO. The Holy Spirit is understood as an eternal divine hypostasis, not as a third subjectivity newly generated by the intersection of two subjectivities. Eastern and Western traditions formulate the procession of the Spirit differently, but neither formulation is immediately equivalent to intersectional emergence in SIO.

In classical Trinitarian ontology, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are eternally their respective hypostases. It does not therefore adopt a developmental structure in which the constitution of the third hypostasis subsequently reconstitutes the modes of existence of Father and Son.

### 4.4.3 Structural Assessment

Because three irreducible hypostases are recognized within indivisible unity, N1 receives partial and interpretation-dependent correspondence. N2 is not satisfied because the Holy Spirit is not defined as a subjectivity emerging from intersection. N3 is also not satisfied because the divine hypostases are immutable.

Within the doctrine, the Holy Spirit is explicitly the third hypostasis and possesses full divine reality. N4 is therefore clearly satisfied. This assessment concerns the ontological reality of the Holy Spirit as the third hypostasis; it does not imply conceptual identity between the Holy Spirit and Intersection Subjectivity in SIO.

The structure is principally a theological ontology of divine being and is not univocally presented as a principle of subjectivity and existence in general. N5 therefore receives partial satisfaction. Since N2 and N3 are not satisfied, N6 is not satisfied.

**Limitation of the present comparison:** This assessment is restricted to representative structures within the Nicene tradition. It does not treat Eastern and Western accounts of procession, hypostasis, relation, or modern social Trinitarian theories as a single homogeneous system.

## 4.5 German Idealism

This section treats Schelling and Hegel as distinct philosophical developments. Both sought to understand nature, spirit, freedom, and being as dynamic structures, but their methods, systems, and later philosophies are not identical. In keeping with the purpose of this chapter, the comparison is limited to these two figures and does not attempt a comprehensive intellectual history of German Idealism.

### 4.5.1 Schelling

#### Textual Evidence

Schelling's philosophy of nature treats nature not as an inert mechanism but as a productive and self-organizing process. The *System of Transcendental Idealism* reconstructs the stages through which self-consciousness comes to understand itself and objectivity. The philosophy of identity seeks to understand subjectivity and objectivity, nature and spirit, within an Absolute irreducible to either side.

The *Freedom Essay*, the drafts of *The Ages of the World*, and the later positive philosophy emphasize ground and existence, freedom, temporal development, and the difference between conceptual possibility and actuality. Schelling's work as a whole cannot therefore be reduced to one completed system.

#### Interpretive Reconstruction

Schelling understands nature not as an inventory of completed substances, but as a dynamic productivity that generates new forms. Subject and object are likewise treated not as two unrelated substances, but as different expressions of a more primordial unity.

At least in the early philosophy and the philosophy of identity, however, this development is explained through the self-manifestation of the Absolute or the productivity of nature. No general principle explicitly states that the constitutive intersection of two differentiated subjectivities generates an irreducible third subjectivity. The later philosophy's criticism of deriving actuality necessarily from concepts does not itself entail the structure of intersection proposed by SIO.

#### Structural Assessment

Dynamic development, differentiation, and the generation of new forms provide partial structural correspondence under N1 and N2, although no third subjectivity is explicit. N3 also remains an interpretation-dependent partial satisfaction with respect to the action of generated forms upon preceding structures. N4 receives partial satisfaction because the ontological status of the candidate third term is not clear. Since Schelling's structure is presented as a general ontology, N5 is clearly satisfied.

None of N1–N4 is judged not satisfied, but their satisfaction and mutual integration remain interpretation-dependent. Under the N6 rule established in Chapter 3, N6 therefore receives partial or interpretation-dependent satisfaction.

**Limitation of the present comparison:** Schelling's philosophy of nature, philosophy of identity, philosophy of freedom, *Ages of the World*, and positive philosophy do not form a single completed system. Their integration across periods is itself an interpretive reconstruction.

### 4.5.2 Hegel

#### Textual Evidence

The *Phenomenology of Spirit* describes a process in which consciousness and self-consciousness develop, through the limitations of their respective standpoints, into recognition, reason, social spirit, and knowledge. In the *Science of Logic*, the determinations of being, essence, and concept develop through their internal dependence and negation. The *Encyclopaedia* presents a system relating logic, nature, and spirit.

In the account of recognition, self-consciousness is constituted through relation to another self-consciousness. In objective spirit, norms, institutions, and social practices shape individual freedom and self-understanding.

#### Interpretive Reconstruction

For Hegel, identity does not arise independently of difference, negation, and mediation. Self-consciousness is likewise not an isolated inner substance, but becomes concrete through recognition by others, language, norms, and social institutions.

Norms and institutions arising from mutual recognition are not reducible to individual intentions and may act back upon individuals. In this respect, the structure partially corresponds to the operational form in SIO whereby a structure constituted through relation transforms the originating subjectivities.

Objective spirit and institutions, however, are not explicitly defined as irreducible third subjectivities emerging from the intersection of two subjectivities. Nor can every dialectical third term be treated as a subjectivity. At the systematic level, development is understood as the self-development of the Concept or Spirit, and its direction of constitution is therefore not identical to that of SIO.

#### Structural Assessment

Recognition, mediation, and objective spirit provide partial and interpretation-dependent correspondence under N1–N3. Because the subjective and ontological status of the candidate third term remains contested, N4 also receives partial satisfaction. The structure is presented as a general philosophical system, so N5 is clearly satisfied.

None of N1–N4 is judged not satisfied, but their correspondence and integration remain interpretation-dependent. N6 therefore receives partial or interpretation-dependent satisfaction.

**Limitation of the present comparison:** This assessment does not resolve disputes among metaphysical, non-metaphysical, social-institutional, and conceptual interpretations of Hegel. Reading objective spirit as a candidate third subjectivity is itself interpretive.

## 4.6 Process Ontology and Phenomenology

This section examines Nishida Kitarō, Alfred North Whitehead, and Maurice Merleau-Ponty. All three criticized the view that isolated substance constitutes the ultimate unit of ontology, but they approached relation and becoming from different starting points: place, event, and body, respectively.

### 4.6.1 Nishida Kitarō

#### Textual Evidence

Nishida's early philosophy centered on pure experience and self-awareness, while his middle period and later work developed the logic of place. Place (*basho*) is understood not as a space containing independently established beings, but as the field in which judgment, subject, object, and individual arise. Absolute nothingness, as the ultimate place, is discussed as encompassing the opposition between being and non-being and between subject and object.

Nishida's later theory of the historical world emphasizes reciprocal determination: individuals form the world while the world conditions their actions and self-understanding. His philosophy developed through several stages, and pure experience, place, absolute nothingness, and the historical world cannot be treated as elements of one unchanged system.

#### Interpretive Reconstruction

In Nishida's structure, self and world do not precede their relation as isolated substances; they arise within the self-determination of place. The reciprocal determination of individual and historical world is not a unidirectional causal relation, but a dynamic structure in which each is a condition for the constitution of the other.

Place or absolute nothingness, however, is not a third subjectivity generated through the intersection of two differentiated subjectivities. It functions instead as the encompassing field that makes possible the differentiation of subject and object, individual and world.

#### Structural Assessment

The relational field and reciprocal determination provide partial correspondence under N1–N3, although the subjective status of the candidate third term is not clear. Place has an ontological role, but not reality as a third subjectivity; N4 therefore receives partial satisfaction. Since the structure is presented as a general ontology, N5 is clearly satisfied.

None of N1–N4 is judged not satisfied, but their correspondence and integration remain interpretation-dependent. N6 therefore receives partial or interpretation-dependent satisfaction.

**Limitation of the present comparison:** Nishida's pure experience, logic of place, absolute nothingness, and theory of the historical world belong to multiple phases of his thought. Mapping reciprocal determination onto a third-subjectivity structure involves interpretive distance.

### 4.6.2 Alfred North Whitehead

#### Textual Evidence

In *Process and Reality*, actual entities or actual occasions are defined not as enduring substances but as processes of becoming. Prehension names the relation through which a new actual occasion takes up and feels elements of antecedent reality. In concrescence, many data are formed into a new unity. Once completed, the actual occasion becomes an objective datum for subsequent becoming.

Enduring objects, living organisms, and persons are understood not as unchanging substrates, but as organized societies or series of actual occasions. Stability is explained through inherited order and repetition rather than through a substance that arrests becoming.

#### Interpretive Reconstruction

In Whitehead's ontology, relation is not something added after the completion of substance. An actual occasion becomes a new subject through its relations with an antecedent world. A structure in which relation generates a new subjective unity is therefore clearly present.

Concrescence, however, ordinarily integrates many antecedent data rather than constituting the intersection of two differentiated subjectivities. Its temporal direction is also asymmetrical: a later actual occasion does not retroactively reconstitute the becoming of antecedent occasions that have already reached completion.

#### Structural Assessment

Because relation gives rise to a new subjective unity, N1 and N2 receive partial correspondence. A later occasion is affected by the antecedent world and conditions subsequent processes, but does not retroactively reconstitute its originating subjects; N3 is therefore not satisfied.

An actual occasion is not merely a relational pattern or an observer's description, but an ontological unit technically defined as a subject in becoming. N4 is therefore clearly satisfied. This assessment concerns its ontological status and does not imply that an actual occasion is identical to Intersection Subjectivity in SIO.

Since the structure is a general ontological principle, N5 is also clearly satisfied. Because N3 is not satisfied, N6 is not satisfied.

**Limitation of the present comparison:** In Whitehead, “subject” is a technical term for the becoming phase of an actual occasion. It cannot be identified without qualification with a human conscious subject or with subjectivity in SIO.

### 4.6.3 Maurice Merleau-Ponty

#### Textual Evidence

The *Phenomenology of Perception* treats the body not merely as an object but as a bodily subject that perceives and acts in the world. In the later *The Visible and the Invisible*, Flesh, chiasm, and reversibility are introduced to describe the relations between seer and seen, toucher and touched, and self and other.

Intercorporeality describes a structure in which embodied subjects belong to a shared sensible world and become mutually intelligible through gesture, action, and perception. Reversibility indicates that activity and passivity, perceiver and perceived, are not two wholly separate domains, while preserving their difference.

#### Interpretive Reconstruction

For Merleau-Ponty, subject and world, self and other, do not first exist as fully independent substances and only subsequently enter into relation. The embodied subject is always already embedded in a perceptual and sensible world. Flesh is the ontological field or element that makes this reversibility and intertwining possible.

Flesh, however, is not a third subjectivity generated through the intersection of two subjectivities. It is instead described as a prior ontological dimension that makes possible the intertwining of multiple subjects and the world.

#### Structural Assessment

N1 is not satisfied because Flesh is not presented as a third subjectivity. Chiasm and mutual constitution provide partial correspondence under N2. Transformation of subjects through relation is discussed, but not as the action of a third subjectivity; N3 is therefore not satisfied. Although Flesh has ontological status, it is not a candidate third subjectivity, so N4 is not satisfied. The relational and embodied structure has broad scope, providing partial correspondence under N5. N6 is not satisfied.

**Limitation of the present comparison:** The later ontology of Flesh and chiasm is found in an unfinished work and working notes. Its systematic position and continuity with the earlier phenomenology remain interpretively uncertain.

## 4.7 Contemporary Relational Theories

This section examines Participatory Sense-Making in cognitive science, the Analytic Third in psychoanalysis, and cosmopsychism in philosophy of mind. The three originate in different disciplines and do not constitute a single theoretical tradition.

### 4.7.1 Participatory Sense-Making

#### Textual Evidence

Participatory Sense-Making, developed by Hanne De Jaegher and Ezequiel Di Paolo, does not explain social cognition solely as inference by isolated individuals concerning the internal states of others. Interaction between autonomous agents may form patterns of coordination and a limited interactional autonomy irreducible to the sum of the participants' intentions.

Participants maintain, modify, or terminate the interaction, while the interaction process that arises may itself enable, constrain, and transform the sense-making of each participant. Autonomy here does not mean a persistent substance independent of the participants, but an interaction process possessing a degree of organization and constraint.

#### Interpretive Reconstruction

On this account, interaction is not merely a channel through which already completed meanings are transmitted. Some meanings arise only through participation, and the interaction process acts back upon individual participants. The structure includes differentiated subjects, constitutive interaction, emergent relational organization, and reciprocal effects.

Interactional autonomy, however, does not imply that the interaction itself becomes a new subjectivity or subject of experience. An autonomous interaction process must be distinguished from an irreducible third subjectivity.

#### Structural Assessment

N1 is not satisfied because the interaction process is not explicitly assigned subjective status. N2 is clearly satisfied insofar as interaction constitutes meaning. N3 is partially satisfied because the process that arises acts back upon the sense-making of the participants. N4 is not satisfied because the candidate third term is not assigned ontological status as a subjectivity. Since the theory's scope is principally social cognition, N5 receives partial satisfaction. Because N1 and N4 are not satisfied, N6 is not satisfied.

**Limitation of the present comparison:** Participatory Sense-Making is a theory of social cognition. Deriving subjectivity as an experiencing subject from interactional autonomy would exceed the theory's own explicit claims.

### 4.7.2 The Analytic Third

#### Textual Evidence

Thomas Ogden's Analytic Third describes an analytic experience that arises through the relation between analyst and analysand and cannot be attributed exclusively to the private experience of either. The analyst's reveries, affects, bodily sensations, associations, and silences may be interpreted both as personal experiences and as expressions of a process co-generated by the analytic relation.

The Analytic Third is not identical to every theory of intersubjectivity or every concept of a psychoanalytic field. Ogden's formulation concerns a particular form of co-generated experience arising within the asymmetrical clinical relation between analyst and analysand.

#### Interpretive Reconstruction

The Analytic Third arises from the relation between two persons with different roles, histories, and responsibilities. It is neither their average nor a symmetrical compromise. The co-generated analytic field exceeds their conscious intentions and alters what each participant can experience and understand.

This structure includes differentiated subjectivities, a co-generated third experiential structure, and effects of that structure upon both participants. The Analytic Third, however, is not presented as a new independent person or enduring substance. Its status as a subjectivity depends upon a clinical and interpretive context.

#### Structural Assessment

Because a third experiential structure is co-generated, N1 receives partial and interpretation-dependent correspondence. N2 is clearly satisfied. N3 is also clearly satisfied because the analytic process changes the experience and understanding of both participants. Although the third possesses clinical reality, it is not explicitly presented as an independent ontological subjectivity; N4 therefore receives partial satisfaction. Since the theory is restricted to the analytic situation, N5 is not satisfied. Because N5 is not satisfied, N6 is also not satisfied.

**Limitation of the present comparison:** Identifying the Analytic Third depends on clinical experience and psychoanalytic interpretation. Extending its ontological status to a general ontology of subjectivity would exceed the scope of the original theory.

### 4.7.3 Cosmopsychism

#### Textual Evidence

Cosmopsychism comprises positions in the philosophy of mind according to which the cosmos as a whole possesses consciousness or fundamental subjectivity. In constitutive and priority cosmopsychism, individual conscious subjects are grounded in, constituted by, or otherwise dependent upon facts concerning a cosmic subject.

This position may avoid the combination problem of explaining how micro-subjects constitute a macro-subject, but it faces the decomposition problem of explaining how multiple finite subjects arise from one cosmic subject. Cosmopsychist theories also differ concerning constitution, grounding, and ontological priority.

#### Interpretive Reconstruction

Cosmopsychism treats subjectivity as ontologically fundamental and directs explanation from whole to individuals. The cosmic subject is not generated from an aggregate of individual subjects; individual subjects depend upon the cosmic subject.

Its direction of constitution is therefore the reverse of SIO's generation of a third subjectivity through the intersection of differentiated subjectivities. The cosmic subject is not a third subjectivity arising between two subjectivities.

#### Structural Assessment

Although the cosmic subject is an irreducible subject, it is not a third subjectivity constituted in relation to two subjectivities. N1 therefore has only limited conceptual similarity and is structurally not satisfied. N2 and N3 are also not satisfied.

Subjectivity is assigned clear ontological reality, but treating the cosmic subject as the candidate third term in a comparison with SIO is itself a limited reconstruction. N4 therefore receives partial satisfaction. Because cosmopsychism is presented as a general ontology of mind, N5 is clearly satisfied. Since N1–N3 are not satisfied, N6 is not satisfied.

**Limitation of the present comparison:** Cosmopsychism is not a single theory. Constitutive cosmopsychism, priority cosmopsychism, and related forms of Russellian monism differ in their accounts of the constitutive relation between whole and individuals.

## 4.8 Methodological Outcome of This Chapter

For each comparison case, this chapter separated textual evidence, interpretive reconstruction, structural assessment, and a case-specific limitation before applying N1–N6 individually.

At this stage, no overall grade is assigned to any theory, and no integrated conclusion is offered concerning historical precedence or proximity to SIO. A case's clear or partial satisfaction of several criteria is recorded as such, but the significance of that combination for the historical position of SIO cannot be concluded from this chapter alone.

Partial satisfaction of N6 likewise does not mean that the complete structure of SIO has been anticipated. It indicates that none of N1–N5 has been found clearly not satisfied, while satisfaction of the individual criteria or their integration within one ontology remains interpretation-dependent. Confirmation of complete structural conjunction requires clear satisfaction of all of N1–N5.

Chapter 5 compares the criterion-level assessments across cases. Only at that stage will the analysis evaluate which structures appear across multiple traditions, which combinations of criteria are concentrated in particular theories, and whether the complete structural conjunction of SIO is identifiable within the scope of the review.

What this chapter has assessed is the structural correspondence of the compared ontologies with SIO, not the truth, validity, or historical novelty of SIO itself. The historical position of SIO is evaluated only in Chapter 5, where the criterion-level assessments obtained here are integrated across cases.
