7. Conclusion

## 7.1 Achievements of This Study

This paper has defined Subjectivity Intersection Ontology (SIO) conceptually, examined its historical position through an explicit comparative methodology, and identified the tasks required for its further philosophical, formal, and empirical development.

Chapter 2 defined the ontological structure of SIO in its own terms, without reducing it to an established philosophical vocabulary. In SIO, subjectivity is defined as perspective. Relative Subjectivity is differentiated through the projection of Absolute Subjectivity, while Intersection Subjectivity emerges in the interval between them. The canonical operational form through which this ontological prototype may be expressed in relation to multiple Relative Subjectivities is:

\[
AB \rightarrow C \rightarrow AB.
\]

In this form, a third subjectivity, C, is constituted through the intersection of differentiated subjectivities A and B, and C acts back upon the subjectivities from which it arose. The canonical operational form does not replace the ontological prototype; it provides a form through which the development of that prototype may be described.

Chapter 3 defined the methodology through which SIO could be compared with other ontologies. It distinguished vocabulary, conceptual, structural, ontological, operational, and empirical levels of comparison, thereby establishing that terminological correspondence cannot by itself demonstrate theoretical identity. It then introduced N1–N5 as independent structural criteria and N6 as the conjunction condition assessing whether those criteria are integrated within a single general ontology. This methodology was presented not as a scale for ranking philosophical value, but as a framework for evaluating criterion-specific structural correspondence with SIO.

Chapter 4 applied this methodology consistently to twenty-one comparison targets. For each target, textual evidence, interpretive reconstruction, and structural assessment were distinguished, and N1–N6 were evaluated according to four categories: clear satisfaction, partial or interpretation-dependent satisfaction, non-satisfaction, and insufficient evidence. The historical significance, religious truth, philosophical superiority, and possible influence upon SIO of the theories examined were outside the scope of these assessments.

Chapter 5 integrated the assessments established in Chapter 4 without modifying them. The analysis showed that individual structures relevant to SIO are distributed across multiple traditions and that some targets exhibit significant partial correspondence across several criteria. No target in the examined comparative set, however, clearly satisfied all of N1–N5 and integrated them within a single general ontology, as required for clear satisfaction of N6. This result was formulated in Proposition 5.1 as the absence of clear structural equivalence within the comparative set.

Chapter 6 examined the philosophical implications and research scope of SIO without deriving its truth or absolute novelty from the comparative findings. It considered the conceptual implications of SIO for subjectivity and objectivity, intersubjectivity, relation, emergence, unity and multiplicity, individuation, temporality, causality, epistemology, and ethics. It also distinguished ontological claims, formal hypotheses, operational indicators, and empirical evidence, and identified the individuation, formalization, empirical discriminability, and failure conditions of third subjectivity as principal tasks for future research.

## 7.2 Central Conclusion

The central structure that characterizes SIO in this paper does not consist in asserting triadicity, relationality, emergence, or interaction separately. It consists in integrating, as a general ontology, a structure in which an irreducible third subjectivity is constituted through the constitutive intersection of differentiated subjectivities and subsequently reconstitutes the subjectivities from which it arose.

The analyses in Chapters 4 and 5 do not show that the elements composing this structure lack historical precedents. Elements corresponding to the ontological fundamentality of subjectivity, differentiation, relational constitution, third terms, generation, reconstitution, and general theoretical scope appear in different forms across multiple traditions examined in this study. Kashmir Śaivism, Schelling, Hegel, and Nishida, moreover, exhibit partial or interpretation-dependent satisfaction of N6.

The conclusion of this paper is therefore not that SIO has no points of correspondence with previous theories. Rather, individual correspondences are distributed across multiple targets, while none of the twenty-one comparison targets examined here clearly exhibits the complete conjunction of N1–N6.

On the basis of this result, SIO cannot, within the examined comparative set, be identified as a merely terminological restatement of any one of the theories considered. This limited distinguishability neither demonstrates complete structural non-equivalence between SIO and the comparison targets nor establishes the absolute historical novelty of SIO.

## 7.3 What This Study Has Not Established and Its Methodological Limitations

This study has not demonstrated the absolute novelty of SIO or its chronological priority within the history of thought. The historical review was restricted to major comparison targets and did not encompass every philosophical, religious, mystical, or scientific tradition. Additional traditions, including Chinese philosophy, Islamic philosophy, Jewish philosophy, and mystical thought, may contain structures warranting further examination.

Nor has this study demonstrated the truth of SIO, its superiority to other ontologies, or the empirical reality of third subjectivity. Historical distinguishability does not entail ontological validity. Likewise, the possibility of presenting a conceptually coherent form does not demonstrate that a corresponding existent is constituted in reality.

This paper has not adopted any particular mathematical model or physical theory as a definitive formalization of SIO. It has also not established specific empirical hypotheses concerning electroencephalography, inter-brain interaction, human–artificial-intelligence interaction, multi-agent systems, or quantum systems. These remain possible domains of application or future hypotheses rather than empirical facts entailed directly by the ontological definition of SIO.

The assessments in Chapter 4 depend upon the selected primary sources, translations, secondary literature, and interpretive reconstructions. Different selections or interpretations may justify revisions to particular N-criterion assessments. The comparative methodology is intended not to eliminate such disagreement, but to identify whether it arises at the level of textual evidence, interpretation, or structural assessment.

N1–N6 are not presented as a universal and final taxonomy for evaluating every ontology. They are SIO-relative criteria designed to examine the extent to which comparison targets correspond structurally to SIO as defined in this paper. The comparative methodology adopted here may itself remain subject to further philosophical and methodological examination. The distinction among levels of comparison, the independence of the individual criteria, the assessment categories, and the conjunction rule governing N6 should all remain open to reevaluation in light of additional comparative cases and critical analysis.

Proposition 5.1 must therefore be understood as a result relative to the specified comparative set, evaluative criteria, and interpretations of the literature. Its scope is limited to the absence of a clearly established structural equivalent among the targets examined; it cannot be treated as a universal demonstration of absence across the history of thought.

## 7.4 Final Position

SIO should accordingly be understood as a structurally distinguishable yet unfinished ontological research program. Its central proposal is that differentiated subjectivities may intersect so as to constitute a further subjectivity irreducible to either of them, and that this generated subjectivity may in turn reconstitute the subjectivities from which it arose.

This paper has defined that proposal without reducing it to any single existing theory, articulated it as a structure capable of systematic comparison, and clarified its position within a delimited historical comparative set. Historical distinctness, however, does not establish ontological validity. Whether SIO can become a viable ontology depends not only upon its historical position, but also upon its conceptual coherence, formal precision, and empirical discriminability.

What this study provides, therefore, is neither the completion nor the confirmation of SIO, but a transparent foundation for the future systematic examination of its conceptual coherence, formalizability, and empirical discriminability. This foundation is not intended to preserve the claims of SIO unconditionally, but to establish a research program capable of clarifying the conditions under which those claims should be refined, tested, revised, or rejected.
