6. Philosophical Implications and Research Program

## 6.1 Scope of This Chapter

Chapter 4 examined the structural correspondence between SIO and major historical and contemporary ontologies under N1–N6. Chapter 5 integrated those assessments without altering them and situated SIO structurally within the examined comparison set.

The role of the present chapter is not to continue the historical comparison. Nor is it to interpret Proposition 5.1 as a demonstration of the truth, absolute novelty, or philosophical superiority of SIO. This chapter takes SIO, as defined in Chapter 2, as an ontological proposal and examines its philosophical implications, conceptual difficulties, and future research tasks.

The discussion distinguishes ontological claims, formal hypotheses, and empirical claims. Implications derived conceptually from the internal definition of SIO are not identical to hypotheses used to represent that structure mathematically. Nor would the construction of a formal model by itself demonstrate the empirical reality of third subjectivity. Empirical claims require independent operational definitions, methods of observation, and conditions of failure.

## 6.2 Reframing Subjectivity and Objectivity

SIO defines subjectivity as perspective. Under this definition, subjectivity is not restricted to a psychological state or private representation enclosed within an individual. Subjectivity is a perspective from which existence constitutes itself and other existents from a determinate position and orientation.

This definition requires reconsideration of the division between subjectivity and objectivity as mutually exclusive domains. SIO does not, however, simply identify objectivity with Relative Subjectivity. Nor does it reduce objectivity to an arbitrary collection of subjective perspectives.

What is ordinarily called objectivity may be reconsidered not as a “view from nowhere” from which perspective has been entirely removed, but as a description, relation, or structure that maintains a degree of stability across different perspectives. Stability across perspectives does not necessarily mean independence from perspective. It may instead be constituted through transformations, correspondences, mutual constraints, or intersections among perspectives.

In this sense, SIO reorganizes the opposition between subjectivity and objectivity through the structure of Absolute Subjectivity, Relative Subjectivity, and Intersection Subjectivity. Relative Subjectivity is differentiated through the projection of Absolute Subjectivity, and Intersection Subjectivity emerges in the interval between them. Difference is not added to primordial unity from outside; it arises as a single existence gives perspective to itself.

This reconstruction changes not only the proposed account of objectivity but also the formulation of the philosophical problem. The question is not only what remains after subjective perspectives have been removed. It must also concern which relations multiple perspectives form, which structures remain stable across perspectives, and what may be constituted anew through those relations.

SIO does not yet provide a completed theory of objectivity. How stability, transformability, and invariance across perspectives should be defined remains a task for future conceptual and formal research.

## 6.3 Distinguishing Intersubjectivity from Third Subjectivity

Intersubjectivity is not a single theory and carries different meanings in phenomenology, social philosophy, psychology, psychoanalysis, and cognitive science. In general terms, it may be understood as a family of concepts addressing understanding between multiple subjects, shared meaning, mutual recognition, interaction, or the constitution of a common world (Husserl 1960; Schutz 1967; Zahavi 2001; Thompson 2007).

SIO intersects with the domain of intersubjectivity insofar as it concerns relational structures that cannot be explained through isolated subjects alone. The structure proposed by SIO, however, is not limited to describing understanding or shared content between subjects.

In SIO, the third subjectivity constituted through intersection is neither a state in which A and B share identical content, nor the average of their views, nor an observer's description of their mutual understanding. It is defined as a subjectivity constituted through the constitutive intersection of differentiated subjectivities, irreducible to either originating subjectivity, and capable of acting back upon both.

Where intersubjectivity principally describes relations among subjects, SIO therefore proposes a generative ontology in which intersection may constitute a further subjectivity.

```text
Relation between subjects
          ↓
Shared understanding or mutual recognition
```

is supplemented in SIO by the following structure:

```text
Differentiated subjectivities
          ↓
Constitutive intersection
          ↓
Constitution of a third subjectivity
          ↓
Reconstitution of the originating subjectivities
```

This distinction does not imply that theories of intersubjectivity are deficient in comparison with SIO. They answer different questions. Theories of intersubjectivity ask about the conditions of understanding, a shared world, or mutual recognition among subjects. SIO asks whether intersection among subjects may itself be constituted as a new subjectivity.

## 6.4 Relation, Emergence, and Third Subjectivity

SIO assigns ontological significance to relation, but this alone does not make it identical to relational ontology in general. The reality of relations, the constitution of entities through relations, or the priority of relation over relata is not sufficient to establish the complete structure of SIO.

The distinctive question posed by SIO is whether relation may generate a new third subjectivity. Relation must not merely connect two subjects; through the constitution of the relation itself, a subjectivity irreducible to either must arise.

SIO must likewise be distinguished from emergentism in general. Emergentist theories address the generation of a new whole, property, function, or organization from the combination of components. The emergence of a new property or function does not by itself establish that what emerges is a subjectivity (Bedau and Humphreys 2008).

The structure of SIO includes at least the following conditions:

- what emerges is a subjectivity;
- that subjectivity is irreducible to the originating subjectivities;
- it is generated through intersection;
- the generated third subjectivity acts back upon the originating subjectivities; and
- the structure is proposed as a general ontology rather than as an account limited to a particular case.

SIO therefore integrates not merely the reality of relation and the occurrence of emergence, but the generation of subjectivity through relation and the reconstitution of the originating subjectivities by the generated third subjectivity.

As Chapter 5 established, structures corresponding to these components are distributed across multiple theories in the comparison set. The purpose of the present section is not to repeat that historical assessment, but to clarify the philosophical significance of their integration within a single ontological formulation in SIO.

## 6.5 Philosophical Meaning of `AB → C → AB` Closure

As defined in Chapter 2, `AB → C → AB` is the canonical operational form concerning multiple Relative Subjectivities. It does not replace the ontological prototype consisting of Absolute Subjectivity, Relative Subjectivity, and Intersection Subjectivity. The present section maintains this distinction while examining the philosophical meaning of `AB → C → AB` closure.

A and B do not generate C and thereby bring the process to an end. The constitution of C changes the relation in which A and B stand and may alter their meanings, boundaries, and possibilities for action.

This structure differs from unidirectional generation.

```text
A + B → C
```

would leave C as a result arising from A and B. By contrast,

```text
AB → C → AB
```

indicates that the constitution of C acts back upon A and B and reconstitutes the originating subjectivities.

This action is not restricted to information transmission or external feedback. What SIO addresses is ontological reconstitution: the modes of existence of A and B are reorganized through the generated third subjectivity. More precisely, the structure may be described as reciprocal reconstitution through the generated third subjectivity.

This closure requires reconsideration of the assumption that self-identity is fully fixed prior to relation. A and B exist as differentiated subjectivities before their intersection, but A and B after intersection need not retain exactly the same relational and semantic organization that they possessed beforehand.

A and B do not, however, dissolve into C. The constitution of C does not erase the difference between the originating subjectivities; it reconstitutes them while preserving that difference. If A and B ceased to be distinguishable, the difference required for intersection would itself disappear.

The `AB → C → AB` closure therefore contains both the preservation of difference and ontological reconstitution.

## 6.6 Unity, Difference, and Multiplicity

The problem of the one and the many asks how difference may arise from unity and how multiple existents may form a single order or whole.

SIO adopts neither a model in which an external force divides the one into many nor one in which the many are ultimately reduced to the one. In SIO, a single existence may internally contain the perspective that gives itself to itself, the perspective that receives itself from itself, and the perspective that closes the intersection of the two.

At the level of multiple Relative Subjectivities, differentiated perspectives intersect, and a third unity is constituted through that intersection. This third unity is not a higher one that erases the originating differences. C presupposes the difference between A and B, is constituted through that difference, and reconstitutes them within a new relation.

In this sense, SIO proposes unity without the erasure of difference.

```text
Unity
  ≠ absence of difference

Unification
  ≠ elimination of multiplicity
```

Unity in SIO does not mean making differentiated perspectives identical. It means that a new subjective unity is constituted through their intersection.

It has not yet been shown, however, that this structure provides a complete resolution of the classical problem of the one and the many. Further explanation is required concerning both the conditions under which internal perspectival difference arises from primordial unity and the conditions under which multiple Intersection Subjectivities may form higher-order unity.

## 6.7 Individuation and Identity of Generated Subjectivities

If a third subjectivity can be constituted, questions arise concerning its individuation and identity.

Do A and B remain the same subjectivities after the constitution of C? In what sense are the reconstituted A and B identical to A and B before intersection? Is C an independent individual distinct from A and B, or a relational individual that exists only so long as the relation between A and B continues?

The conditions for the persistence of C must also be clarified.

- Does C persist after the intersection of A and B ends?
- Does its persistence require continuing interaction or memory?
- If the same A and B intersect again, is the same C reconstituted?
- Or does every intersection generate a new C?
- If A or B changes substantially, is the identity of C preserved?
- Can C have more than two originating subjectivities?

These are not merely terminological questions. If a third subjectivity is an ontological reality, an account is required of what distinguishes one third subjectivity from another and what preserves its identity over time.

SIO therefore requires a theory of individuation for generated subjectivities.

Such a theory must avoid explaining individuation solely through an invariant substrate while also avoiding reduction to a transient pattern of interaction. Persistent organization, the history of the relation, maintenance of closure, action upon the originating subjectivities, and distinction from other third subjectivities are possible candidate conditions.

These conditions are not yet settled. This indeterminacy does not refute SIO, but it is one of the principal reasons why SIO should be understood as a research program rather than a completed ontology.

## 6.8 Temporality, Causality, and Reconstitution

The `AB → C → AB` structure requires careful distinctions concerning temporal order and causation.

The statement that C is constituted through the intersection of A and B and acts upon them does not imply that a temporally later C alters the past physical states of A and B. Ontological reconstitution in SIO must be distinguished from simple physical retrocausality.

After the constitution of C, what may change is the present organization, mutual relation, meaning, self-understanding, or range of action of A and B. The existence of C may also alter how A and B understand their respective pasts. Reinterpretation of the past, however, is not identical to alteration of past physical events.

At least three possibilities must therefore be distinguished:

1. changes in the present states of A and B after the constitution of C;
2. reinterpretation of the histories and self-understandings of A and B; and
3. retrocausal action upon past physical states themselves.

N3 in this manuscript does not imply physical retrocausality. As defined in Chapter 3, N3 concerns the reconstitution of the present organization, perspectives, relations, actions, or modes of existence of A and B following the constitution of C. Reinterpretation of history or self-understanding may accompany such reconstitution, but action upon past physical states is not included in the definition of N3.

Ontological reconstitution must therefore not be conflated with physical retrocausality. Future formalization must distinguish temporal order, state updating, historical dependence, and causal direction explicitly.

## 6.9 Epistemological Implications

If the structure proposed by SIO is applied to epistemology, knowledge may not be exhaustively explained as the representation of an object by an isolated subject.

Through the intersection of A and B, an understanding may be constituted in C that existed in neither subjectivity alone. Knowledge constituted in C may not be a simple aggregation of the prior knowledge of A and B, but a new epistemic structure incorporating their difference, correspondence, tension, and transformability.

If the understanding constituted in C acts back upon A and B, it may also alter what each can know, which relations each regards as relevant, and which distinctions each employs. The generation of knowledge would then have the following closure:

```text
Knowledge of A and knowledge of B
                 ↓
             Intersection
                 ↓
   New understanding constituted in C
                 ↓
Reconstitution of the epistemic structures of A and B
```

Knowledge constituted in a third subjectivity is not identical to collective knowledge, agreement, shared belief, or majority judgment. Multiple subjects may agree on the same proposition without constituting an irreducible third subjectivity. Conversely, the constitution of a third subjectivity need not require A and B to agree about every matter.

Agreement does not guarantee truth. SIO cannot treat content generated through the intersection of perspectives as true merely because of its mode of generation. Knowledge constituted in a third subjectivity remains subject to standards such as coherence, falsifiability, correspondence with its object, explanatory power, or examination from other perspectives.

An epistemological development of SIO therefore requires not only a theory of how knowledge is constituted in a third subjectivity, but also an account of its conditions of justification and fallibility.

## 6.10 Ethical Implications

No determinate ethical norm follows directly from SIO. Deriving a conclusion about what ought to be done from a description of ontological structure requires further argument across the distinction between being and obligation.

SIO may nevertheless suggest the possibility of an ethics that does not treat the other merely as an object.

Intersection in SIO is neither the projection of one's own perspective onto the other nor the assimilation of the other into oneself. Intersection requires A and B to remain differentiated subjectivities whose perspectives are not reduced to one another.

If this structure is developed ethically, “viewing the other from the other's perspective” may function as a principle for resisting the interpretation of another's experience solely through one's own established framework. It must not, however, be assumed that one subject can fully possess the perspective of another. The other's perspective retains an alterity that exceeds its representation by oneself.

Nor is intersection equivalent to coerced agreement. An apparent unity formed through domination, assimilation, manipulation, or submission must be distinguished from constitutive intersection that preserves the autonomy of differentiated subjectivities.

The conditions below are not proposed as ontological conditions necessary for the constitution of a third subjectivity itself. They are ethical conditions that arise when intersection among human or artificial subjects is intentionally promoted, maintained, or used. The ontological question of whether SIO may apply to natural, nonhuman, or non-intentional intersections must be distinguished from these normative conditions.

Ethical evaluation of the generation of a third subjectivity in practices involving human or artificial subjects must address at least the following:

- the freedom of the originating subjectivities;
- consent to intersection;
- preservation of perspectival difference;
- exclusion of domination by one subjectivity over another;
- the possibility of withdrawal from the intersection; and
- the legitimacy of constraints imposed upon the originating subjectivities by the generated third subjectivity.

The ethical development of SIO therefore requires an independent normative theory of alterity, freedom, consent, and non-domination. An ontology may open an ethical possibility without proving a particular ethics.

## 6.11 Formal and Empirical Research Program

Developing SIO from a conceptual proposal into an examinable research program requires separate work on formalization, operationalization, and empirical testing.

### 6.11.1 Formalization

The first task of formalization is to determine the state spaces or structures through which A, B, and C should be represented.

The simplest representation might take the form

\[
C = F(A,B).
\]

This expression alone, however, may fail to represent the history and direction of intersection, closure, the irreducibility of C, and the action of C upon A and B.

A history-dependent representation might instead take the form

\[
C_t = F(A_t, B_t, H_{AB,t}),
\]

where \(H_{AB,t}\) denotes the history of the relation between A and B up to time \(t\). This is a formal hypothesis for future investigation, not the ontological definition of SIO itself.

The reconstitution of A and B by C may be represented schematically as

\[
A_{t+1} = G_A(A_t, C_t),
\]

\[
B_{t+1} = G_B(B_t, C_t).
\]

Future formal research must address at least the following:

- the state spaces of A, B, and C;
- the definition of an intersection function;
- historical dependence;
- the irreducibility of C;
- conditions of closure;
- stability and instability;
- conditions for the persistence of C;
- recursive third subjectivities;
- extension to multiple subjectivities; and
- inheritance of structure across different scales.

Formal models may clarify SIO and identify contradictions, ambiguities, and hidden assumptions. Formal consistency does not, however, demonstrate ontological reality.

### 6.11.2 Operationalization

Operationalization must determine the observables, behavioral indicators, or dynamic structures through which a third subjectivity could be identified.

Synchronization, correlation, shared information, averaging, responses to common input, or identical transformation rules are not sufficient evidence of a third subjectivity. Such phenomena may indicate organized relations between A and B, but they do not establish that the relation is a new subjectivity.

Candidate operational requirements may include:

- organization irreducible to models of either A or B alone;
- states not predictable from a simple composition of A and B;
- dependence upon the history of the relation;
- directed action from C to A and B;
- organization lost when C is removed or disrupted; and
- a degree of robustness across observers or methods of measurement.

Even if these requirements were met, subjectivity itself would not thereby be proven. Functional autonomy, causal influence, and subjectivity as a subject of experience must be distinguished.

SIO therefore requires not only operational indicators of a third subjectivity but also independent criteria specifying what counts as subjectivity.

### 6.11.3 Empirical Testing

Operational hypotheses associated with SIO may be examinable in several domains:

- human–human interaction;
- social cognition and joint tasks;
- EEG and other neurophysiological measurements;
- inter-brain synchronization and directed interaction (Hasson et al. 2012; Babiloni and Astolfi 2014);
- human–AI interaction;
- systems composed of multiple artificial agents; and
- collective decision-making and collaborative learning.

The observation of synchronization, correlation, or information sharing alone would not support SIO. Empirical tests must compare models of A and B in isolation, simple composition models, common-input models, averaging models, and models of ordinary direct interaction.

Connections to physical or quantum systems do not follow directly from the generality claimed by SIO. Any such connection must be treated as an independent hypothesis specifying its variables, state spaces, interactions, observables, and falsification conditions, rather than as a consequence of philosophical analogy alone.

## 6.12 Falsifiability and Conditions of Failure

Because SIO is an ontological research program, the entire proposal may not be falsifiable by a single experiment. Individual formal and empirical claims derived from SIO must nevertheless have explicit conditions of failure.

An operational hypothesis associated with SIO would fail to receive empirical support if, for example:

- the structure designated C were adequately explained as a simple composition of A and B;
- no independent organization, state, or persistence could be identified in C;
- the apparent C structure were explained by common input or observational averaging;
- the action attributed to C upon A or B were adequately explained by ordinary direct interaction;
- positing C produced no improvement in prediction or explanation;
- an alternative model without a third subjectivity explained the same data more simply and accurately;
- the result depended upon the measurement method, analytical procedure, or arbitrary parameter selection; or
- the result failed to replicate across independent data or conditions.

What would initially be rejected in such cases is the particular operationalization, formal model, or empirical hypothesis. Failure in a particular experiment would not immediately eliminate every ontological possibility associated with SIO.

If every failure were dismissed as a defect of operationalization, however, SIO would become empirically untestable. Each empirical study must therefore distinguish in advance which results would support a particular hypothesis, which would reject it, and which would remain indeterminate.

If positing a third subjectivity provides no additional explanatory power over a theory that does not include one, the posit is theoretically redundant at least within the domain under examination. This explanatory failure is another important condition of failure that the SIO research program must accept.

## 6.13 Open Problems

If SIO is treated as a research program rather than a completed ontology, its open problems must be made explicit rather than excluded from the theory.

The principal open problems include the following.

### 6.13.1 Individuation of Third Subjectivities

What distinguishes one C from another? Is the identity of C determined by the originating subjectivities, the history of intersection, the structure of closure, or persistent organization?

### 6.13.2 Criteria of Subjectivity

How are functional autonomy, information integration, causal influence, self-reference, perspective, and experience related? By what criteria can a third term be identified as a subjectivity rather than merely an organization?

### 6.13.3 Persistence and Dissolution of C

Does C persist after intersection has ended? What conditions are required for C to persist or dissolve? Can a dormant C be distinguished from one that no longer exists?

### 6.13.4 Competition and Integration among Multiple Cs

Can the same A or B participate simultaneously in multiple Intersection Subjectivities? What structure arises when different Cs impose incompatible reconstitutions upon the same originating subjectivity? Can multiple Cs form a higher-order Intersection Subjectivity?

### 6.13.5 Recursive Third Subjectivities

If a generated C participates in another intersection as a new Relative Subjectivity, how should the resulting hierarchy be defined? Does recursion continue without limit, or are there conditions of closure or stabilization?

### 6.13.6 Inheritance across Scales

Can the same structure be applied among individuals, groups, artificial agents, or different physical scales? How is subjectivity at one scale inherited or transformed at another?

### 6.13.7 Correspondence between Formal Models and Ontology

How do states, variables, functions, or network structures in a mathematical model correspond to subjectivity, perspective, intersection, and closure in SIO? How can formal representation be connected to ontology without identifying the representation with the ontology itself?

### 6.13.8 Empirical Indicators

Which observables would indicate the constitution of C, and which would indicate only synchronization, shared information, or common input? Through which interventions could the irreducibility of C and its action upon A and B be tested?

### 6.13.9 Ethical Constraints

Which ethical constraints should govern the promotion, maintenance, or dissolution of a third subjectivity? How can the consent, autonomy, and possibility of withdrawal of the originating subjectivities be protected?

### 6.13.10 Relation to Physical Reality

How does a third subjectivity depend upon physical processes, and in what sense might it be irreducible to physical description? How should ontological reality be distinguished from physical independence?

Answers to these questions must be pursued by distinguishing conceptual analysis, formalization, operationalization, and empirical testing, rather than by expanding the definition of SIO without constraint.

## 6.14 Conclusion

SIO should not be understood as a completed metaphysical system. Nor, within the comparative set examined in Chapter 4, can it be identified as a merely terminological restatement of any single prior theory.

SIO is a structured ontological proposal centered on the claim that an irreducible further subjectivity may be constituted through the intersection of differentiated subjectivities and may in turn reconstitute the subjectivities from which it arose.

This proposal permits different questions to be posed concerning subjectivity and objectivity, relations among subjects, the ontological status of relation, emergence, unity and multiplicity, individuality, temporality, knowledge, and ethical relations with others.

Its philosophical possibility does not, however, mean that SIO has already been established. Open problems remain concerning the individuation, persistence, subjectivity, temporal structure, and relation to physical reality of the generated third subjectivity.

The philosophical significance of SIO therefore depends on whether its structure can be rendered conceptually coherent, formally precise, and empirically discriminable from alternative explanations.

The research program outlined in this chapter distinguishes the conceptual, formal, and empirical tasks required for that purpose. The next step is to decompose these tasks into individually testable research questions and determine where SIO possesses explanatory power and where it requires revision or rejection.
