# 5.6 Contemporary Relational Ontologies

## Introduction

During the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries, several influential theories shifted attention away from models of cognition centered on isolated individuals. Across cognitive science, psychoanalysis, and philosophy of mind, relation, interaction, participation, and collective organization came to play increasingly important explanatory roles. These developments did not produce a unified relational ontology, but they generated new ways of describing how subjects, meanings, and organized wholes depend upon relations.

Participatory Sense-Making, the Analytic Third, and Cosmopsychism represent three distinct attempts to reconsider the ontological significance of relation. Participatory Sense-Making is an enactive account of social cognition. The Analytic Third is a psychoanalytic concept developed within clinical theory and practice. Cosmopsychism is a family of positions in contemporary philosophy of mind concerning the priority of consciousness at the level of the cosmos. Their disciplinary origins, explanatory aims, and units of analysis differ substantially.

The purpose of this section is therefore not to combine them into a single research tradition. It is to evaluate their structural similarities to and differences from Subjectivity Intersection Ontology (SIO), the comparison framework introduced in this paper. The comparison does not imply historical dependence and is limited to the conjunction specified by N1–N6.

## 5.6.1 Participatory Sense-Making

### Historical Background

Participatory Sense-Making was introduced by Hanne De Jaegher and Ezequiel Di Paolo within the enactive approach to social cognition. Enactivism understands cognition as embodied sense-making performed by autonomous agents in active relation with their environments. It rejects the assumption that cognition is adequately explained as the internal representation of an independently given world.

The extension of enactivism to social cognition addressed a specific limitation in individual-centered accounts. Social understanding cannot always be reduced to one agent observing, simulating, or inferring the hidden mental states of another. The interaction process may acquire patterns, constraints, and transitions that depend upon both participants while remaining reducible to neither participant's intentions considered in isolation.

Participatory Sense-Making describes the coordination of intentional activity through which individual processes of sense-making are modified and, in some cases, new domains of meaning become available. It is not intended to eliminate individual agency. Its central problem is how autonomous agents and an interaction process can jointly organize social cognition without either level being reduced to the other.

### Ontological Structure

Autonomy has a technical role in the enactive framework. An autonomous system maintains an identity through a network of mutually enabling processes. In social interaction, a coordinated relational process may also develop a limited form of autonomy. A conversation, joint activity, or patterned encounter can acquire a momentum and organization that neither participant fully controls.

Coordination is necessary but not sufficient for participatory sense-making. Agents must regulate aspects of their coupling in ways that affect the continuation and organization of the interaction itself. The interaction may then constrain the participants while the participants continue to sustain, redirect, or terminate it. Interactional autonomy remains dependent upon the activity of the individuals and does not erase their own autonomy.

Meaning is generated and transformed through this interplay. A participant's sense-making can be enabled, redirected, or restricted by the other's activity and by the emerging organization of the encounter. Some meanings arise only through participation and are not available to either individual in the same form outside that interaction. Relation is therefore constitutive of at least some forms of social cognition rather than merely a causal channel by which already completed meanings are exchanged.

The theory remains methodologically tied to embodied cognitive science. Its claims concern agents, coordination, sense-making, and interaction dynamics. Although these concepts have ontological implications, Participatory Sense-Making does not by itself offer a comprehensive ontology of all subjectivity or existence.

### Relation to SIO

Participatory Sense-Making is one of the closest contemporary interaction-based comparisons to SIO because it treats interaction as constitutive rather than merely causal. It begins from differentiated autonomous agents, explains how their regulated coupling can produce an emergent relational organization, and allows that organization to constrain and transform the participants' sense-making. These features correspond closely to N2 and partially to N3.

The interaction process, however, is described as an autonomous organization in the relational domain, not explicitly as an irreducible ontological subjectivity. It may generate meanings that belong to the interactional process, but the theory does not ordinarily claim that the interaction itself becomes a subject with a standpoint equivalent to or distinct from those of the participants. Interactional autonomy and subjectivity are not interchangeable concepts.

Participatory Sense-Making consequently provides strong support for the intelligibility of constitutive interaction without clearly satisfying N1 or N4. Its general applicability within social cognition supports a broad theoretical scope, but it does not formulate the complete SIO conjunction as a general ontology.

## 5.6.2 Analytic Third

### Historical Background

Thomas Ogden introduced the concept of the Analytic Third within psychoanalytic accounts of intersubjectivity and the analytic process. The concept was developed to describe dimensions of experience that arise through the relation between analyst and analysand and that cannot be assigned exclusively to either participant. Ogden's clinical discussions attend to the analyst's reverie, bodily experience, affect, and apparently private trains of thought as possible expressions of an intersubjectively generated process.

This proposal belongs to a broader transformation within psychoanalysis in which the analytic encounter came to be understood less as the observation of one isolated psyche by a neutral external interpreter. Relational and intersubjective approaches emphasized that analyst and analysand participate in a field that shapes what can be experienced, repeated, interpreted, and communicated.

The Analytic Third is nevertheless a specific formulation within this wider landscape. It should not be treated as identical with every concept of intersubjectivity, field theory, or relational unconscious. Nor does it imply that the analyst's and analysand's subjectivities disappear into a collective mind.

### Ontological Structure

The analytic third emerges through the interaction of two differentiated participants. It names an intersubjectively generated mode of experience formed by the analytic pair. The analyst and analysand contribute asymmetrically because they occupy different roles, possess different histories, and have different responsibilities within the clinical setting. The resulting third is therefore neither a simple sum nor a symmetrical compromise between their perspectives.

A shared analytic field is co-created through speech, silence, affect, fantasy, bodily response, expectation, and unconscious patterning. What occurs in that field can exceed the conscious intentions of either participant. The analytic third becomes accessible only through the situated experiences of analyst and analysand; it is not observed from a neutral position outside their relation.

The concept also has a recursive dimension. The intersubjective process shapes what each participant experiences and can understand. The analyst's reverie, for example, may be partly personal and partly an expression of the analytic field. Interpretation can alter the field, which in turn changes the subsequent experience of both participants. This reciprocity makes the analytic third a strong example of an emergent relational structure with feedback effects.

Its status must be stated carefully. Ogden's language of a third does not require positing a separately existing person or a new enduring substance. The analytic third is a clinical and intersubjective reality whose identification depends upon interpretation within a particular analytic relationship. Its ontological implications are therefore substantial but not identical with a general metaphysical claim.

### Relation to SIO

The Analytic Third provides one of the strongest historical examples of an emergent third relational structure. It begins with differentiated subjectivities, locates the third in their co-created experience, and describes how the emergent analytic process can reorganize each participant's experience. These features create close partial correspondences to N1–N3.

The correspondence remains limited. The analytic third is formulated within psychoanalytic practice and addresses a specific dyad organized by clinical roles, techniques, and aims. It is not presented as a general ontology of subjectivity applicable independently of the analytic setting. Its status as a “subject” is also interpretation-dependent: the concept identifies an intersubjectively generated mode of experience rather than unambiguously asserting a distinct ontological subjectivity.

Accordingly, the Analytic Third strongly satisfies the generative aspect represented by N2 and partially approaches N1 and N3. It does not clearly satisfy N4, N5, or the complete conjunction required by N6.

## 5.6.3 Cosmopsychism

### Historical Background

Cosmopsychism is a family of positions in contemporary philosophy of mind according to which the cosmos as a whole is conscious or possesses a fundamental form of subjectivity. It reverses the more familiar explanatory direction of views that begin with micro-level subjects and attempt to derive human or other macroscopic consciousness through their combination.

Some versions of cosmopsychism are developed in relation to Russellian monism. Russellian monists distinguish the structural and dispositional properties described by physical theory from the intrinsic nature that realizes those structures. Consciousness-involving properties may be proposed as candidates for that intrinsic nature. Cosmopsychism places the fundamental consciousness-involving reality at the cosmic rather than the micro-level.

Constitutive cosmopsychism holds, in broad terms, that facts about individual conscious subjects are grounded in or constituted by consciousness-involving facts about the cosmos. Priority cosmopsychism emphasizes the ontological priority of the conscious whole over its parts. These formulations overlap but should not be assumed to make identical grounding claims.

### Ontological Structure

The universe is treated as the fundamental subject or bearer of consciousness, while individual minds are derivative in some relevant sense. This structure combines subjectivity-first ontology with a whole-to-parts order of explanation. The cosmic subject is not produced through the aggregation of individual minds; individual minds must instead be explained in relation to the fundamental conscious whole.

One motivation for cosmopsychism is the combination problem facing micropsychist forms of panpsychism. If fundamental micro-level entities are already subjects of experience, it is difficult to explain how many such subjects could constitute one macrosubject. Cosmopsychism avoids beginning with subject combination because it posits a conscious cosmos from the outset.

The reversal creates a corresponding difficulty. A theory must explain how one cosmic subject grounds, constitutes, or otherwise gives rise to many distinct finite subjects. This is often described as the decombination problem. It is not resolved merely by asserting the priority of the whole. A complete cosmopsychist theory must specify how distinct perspectives and boundaries arise within cosmic subjectivity without being either illusory or independent of the fundamental whole.

Relations between whole and parts are therefore central, but they are not relations among initially differentiated subjects whose interaction generates a new subject. The explanatory movement proceeds from one fundamental subject toward derivative individuals. Cosmopsychism is consequently a subject-centered ontology, but it is not primarily an interaction-centered one.

### Relation to SIO

Cosmopsychism shares SIO's attribution of fundamental ontological significance to subjectivity. It also rejects the assumption that individual minds are the only or most basic subjects and permits a subject whose scope exceeds any particular individual. In this limited respect, the cosmic subject supplies a partial comparison to an irreducible subjectivity that cannot be reduced to individual viewpoints.

The direction of explanation is nevertheless opposite to the generative direction specified by SIO. Cosmopsychism begins with the cosmic subject and seeks to account for individual minds as grounded in, constituted by, or dependent upon it. SIO begins with differentiated subjectivities and proposes that their constitutive intersection may generate an irreducible third subjectivity. The cosmic subject is not the product of such an intersection.

Cosmopsychism therefore clearly supports N4 and N5 but does not satisfy N2 or N3. Its partial proximity to N1 concerns the irreducibility and expanded scale of subjectivity, not the emergence of a third from a relation between two subjects.

## Interim Verdict

Contemporary relational theories substantially narrow the historical distance between traditional ontology and the structure evaluated as SIO. They make interaction, intersubjective emergence, and fundamental subjectivity available in conceptually developed forms while locating those forms in different disciplinary contexts.

Participatory Sense-Making provides the strongest interaction-based comparison considered in this section. It explains how regulated coupling can become an emergent autonomous organization that participates in the generation and transformation of meaning. The Analytic Third provides the strongest emergent relational comparison: an intersubjectively generated process can exceed either participant and recursively shape both. Cosmopsychism provides one of the strongest contemporary subject-centered ontologies by treating subjectivity at the cosmic level as fundamental.

None of the three, however, explicitly formulates the complete conjunction of:

- differentiated subjectivities;
- constitutive intersection;
- an irreducible emergent third subjectivity; and
- recursive reconstruction of the originating subjectivities

as a general ontological framework. Participatory Sense-Making does not ordinarily attribute subjectivity to the autonomous interaction process. The Analytic Third remains a clinical and interpretive construct of psychoanalytic practice rather than a general ontology. Cosmopsychism proceeds from a fundamental cosmic subject toward individual minds rather than from interaction among differentiated subjects.

These theories are accordingly treated as important contemporary predecessors or proximate structural comparisons, not as complete formulations of SIO. This classification concerns the N1–N6 framework alone and does not evaluate their explanatory adequacy within their respective disciplines.
