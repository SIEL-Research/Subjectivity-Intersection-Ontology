# Appendix A

## Methodology for Comparative Ontology

## A.1 Motivation

Comparative ontology has traditionally depended upon qualitative historical interpretation. Scholars reconstruct concepts within their linguistic, argumentative, and historical settings and then assess similarities and differences across philosophical traditions. This approach has produced philosophically substantial scholarship and remains indispensable to any responsible comparison of ontological systems.

In many comparative studies, however, the criteria governing judgments of similarity remain partly implicit. Two theories may be described as “close,” “relational,” or “triadic” without a prior statement of which structural features are decisive. As a result, disagreements about a particular comparison can become disagreements about the comparison as a whole. Differences in terminology, source selection, translation, and interpretive background may further obscure the basis of an evaluation.

The method used in this paper introduces explicit structural criteria in order to improve transparency and reproducibility. Transparency requires the grounds for each comparative judgment to be stated in advance and applied consistently. Reproducibility, in this context, does not imply that historical interpretation can be made mechanical or that competent readers must reach identical conclusions. It means that another reader can reconstruct the evaluative procedure, identify the textual or interpretive premises behind a judgment, and determine where a disagreement arises.

The framework therefore supplements rather than replaces qualitative historical interpretation. It depends upon historically informed reading to determine what a theory claims, while making the subsequent act of structural comparison more explicit and auditable.

## A.2 Structural Evaluation Criteria

The comparison is organized around six criteria, designated N1–N6. They isolate the components of the ontological structure proposed as SIO and ask whether those components appear in each theory under review:

- **N1: Irreducible Third Subjectivity.** Whether a third term related to two subjects is treated as a subjectivity irreducible to their sum, average, or descriptive representation.
- **N2: Generation Through Intersection.** Whether the third subjectivity is generated through the constitutive intersection of differentiated subjectivities rather than existing prior to their relation.
- **N3: Recursive Reconstruction.** Whether the emergent third subjectivity subsequently transforms, reorganizes, or reconstitutes the originating subjects.
- **N4: Ontological Reality.** Whether the third subjectivity is presented as ontologically real rather than solely epistemological, psychological, sociological, linguistic, or metaphorical.
- **N5: General Theoretical Scope.** Whether the structure is proposed beyond a single person, dyad, culture, religion, community, or exceptional case and is capable of functioning as a general ontological principle.
- **N6: Complete Structural Conjunction.** Whether one theory integrates differentiated subjectivities, constitutive intersection, an irreducible emergent third subjectivity, and recursive reconstruction in a self-consistent structure.

The criteria are not intended to measure philosophical value. A theory may be historically influential, conceptually rigorous, or explanatorily powerful while receiving a low similarity assessment under this framework. Conversely, a high degree of structural similarity does not establish the truth or adequacy of either theory.

The criteria serve a narrower purpose: to determine whether multiple components are integrated within a single theoretical framework. N6 is therefore conjunctive. It cannot be satisfied by collecting separate features from different texts, periods, or traditions unless those features belong to a defensibly unified theory.

## A.3 Comparative Procedure

The comparative procedure consists of five stages.

1. **Historical literature review.** Each comparison begins with an examination of representative primary texts and relevant scholarship. The aim is to reconstruct the theory within its historical vocabulary before translating its structure into the terms of the comparative framework. Where a tradition contains multiple schools, periods, or contested interpretations, those differences must be stated rather than collapsed into a single position.

2. **Identification of ontological structures.** The review then identifies the theory's relevant commitments concerning substance, process, relation, subjectivity, emergence, plurality, and transformation. This stage distinguishes verbal resemblance from structural correspondence. The presence of a “third,” for example, does not establish that it is a subjectivity, that it is generated through intersection, or that it recursively affects its originating terms.

3. **Evaluation using N1–N6.** Each theory is assessed separately against the six criteria. The procedure distinguishes clear satisfaction, partial or interpretation-dependent satisfaction, non-satisfaction, and insufficient evidence. A partial judgment is appropriate where a correspondence depends upon a disputed interpretation, applies only within a restricted domain, or captures some but not all of the relevant structure.

4. **Overall Similarity Grade.** The criterion-level assessment is summarized by a qualitative grade from S to D. S indicates structural equivalence in the dimensions evaluated; A indicates very close similarity with at least one decisive component absent; B records important shared structures but multiple missing criteria; C indicates limited or indirect similarity; and D identifies a fundamental difference under the selected dimensions. The grade is a summary judgment and does not replace the individual N1–N6 results.

5. **Novelty Verdict.** A concise verdict identifies the type of historical relation disclosed by the comparison, such as a process, relational, triadic, phenomenological, psychological, or functional predecessor. The verdict is intended to preserve the specific contribution of the theory under review while distinguishing partial anticipation from complete structural conjunction.

Each stage is designed to make disagreement local rather than global. A reader may dispute the interpretation of a source, the application of one criterion, the overall grade, or the wording of a verdict without rejecting the entire comparative procedure. Revisions can therefore be made at the level where the disagreement occurs.

This localization does not eliminate interpretive judgment. The selection of representative texts, the translation of technical terms, and the determination of structural equivalence remain philosophical tasks. The method makes those judgments more visible; it does not convert them into value-neutral measurements.

## A.4 Methodological Scope

The framework presented here is one possible methodology for comparative ontology. It is not proposed as the only legitimate form of historical or cross-traditional analysis. Genealogical study, conceptual history, philology, contextualism, comparative phenomenology, and problem-centered reconstruction may disclose relations that the N1–N6 framework does not capture.

The method is also limited by the scope of the corpus. A judgment that no equivalent formulation was identified among the theories reviewed does not establish absence from the philosophical record as a whole. It remains possible that additional texts, underexamined traditions, or alternative interpretations will change an individual assessment or the overall historical conclusion.

The criteria themselves reflect the research question of this paper. A study concerned with causation, normativity, temporality, embodiment, or political ontology would require a different set of dimensions. Future studies may revise the definitions, add or remove criteria, alter the categories of satisfaction, or replace the Similarity Grade and Novelty Verdict with other evaluative procedures.

Comparison across traditions also requires caution concerning conceptual translation. Terms such as “subject,” “person,” “self,” “consciousness,” and “relation” do not possess identical meanings in every historical context. Structural comparison is warranted only when such differences are retained rather than treated as terminological obstacles to be ignored.

## A.5 Open Questions

The methodology remains open to refinement through future philosophical discussion. Several questions require further examination.

First, the independence of the criteria should be assessed. N6 deliberately summarizes a conjunction of other structural elements, while N1–N5 may also overlap in particular cases. Further analysis may determine whether the criteria should be treated as logically independent dimensions, hierarchically ordered requirements, or parts of a more formal dependency structure.

Second, the basis of partial satisfaction requires additional specification. A triangle may currently represent interpretive uncertainty, restricted scope, functional analogy, or incomplete structural correspondence. Distinguishing these forms of partiality could improve the precision of the matrix.

Third, the relationship between criterion-level judgments and the overall Similarity Grade remains qualitative. Alternative methods might introduce explicit decision rules, weighted criteria, confidence assessments, or separate grades for historical, functional, and ontological proximity. Any such formalization would need to avoid creating a misleading appearance of measurement precision.

Fourth, the evidential threshold for claims of historical novelty must remain proportionate to the corpus examined. A transparent procedure can support a provisional conclusion within a defined review; it cannot establish universal absence without a correspondingly comprehensive body of evidence.

These questions do not invalidate the comparative framework. They identify the conditions under which it may be clarified, criticized, and revised. The methodology should therefore be treated as an open research instrument whose adequacy depends upon continued application and assessment.
