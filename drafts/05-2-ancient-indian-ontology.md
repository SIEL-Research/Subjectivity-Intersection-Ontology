# 5.2 Ancient Indian Ontological Alternatives

## Introduction

Ancient Indian philosophy developed one of the world's most extensive and internally diverse traditions of ontological inquiry, largely independently of the Greek lineages considered in the preceding section. Indian thinkers formulated systematic accounts of consciousness, selfhood, substance, causation, dependence, plurality, change, and liberation. Although intellectual exchange across regions occurred at several historical points, the major Indian debates examined here arose from textual, ritual, and argumentative contexts with their own concepts and standards of analysis.

This section does not attempt a comprehensive survey of Indian philosophy. It selects several ontological alternatives that bear directly on the framework introduced in this paper as Subjectivity Intersection Ontology (SIO). The relevant question is whether a tradition posits differentiated subjectivities whose constitutive intersection generates an irreducible third subjectivity, and whether that emergent subjectivity recursively reconstitutes the subjects from which it arises.

The traditions considered below differ profoundly from one another and should not be treated as parts of a single unified system. They also span a long historical period. The Upaniṣads belong to ancient India, while systematic Advaita Vedānta and the principal philosophical formulations of Kashmir Śaivism are later developments, the latter flourishing primarily from the ninth to the eleventh centuries CE. The heading of this section therefore designates a broad history of Indian ontological alternatives rather than a narrowly bounded chronological period.

## 5.2.1 Upanishadic and Advaita Ontology

The early Upaniṣads develop several accounts of the self (*ātman*) and ultimate reality (*brahman*). In some passages, *ātman* denotes the innermost principle of the person; in others, inquiry into the self becomes inseparable from inquiry into the ground or totality of reality. Formulations associating or identifying *ātman* and *brahman* became especially important for later Vedānta. The Upaniṣads are not, however, a single systematic treatise, and their claims about unity, difference, cosmology, and personhood should not be collapsed into one uniform doctrine.

Advaita Vedānta gives a systematic nondual interpretation to selected Upaniṣadic teachings. In its classical formulation, most prominently associated with Śaṅkara, the true self is not the empirical individual considered as body, biography, or mental complex. It is pure consciousness, ultimately identical with *brahman*, the nondual reality of all things. Ignorance produces the attribution of limiting characteristics to the self and sustains the experienced division among subjects and objects. Liberating knowledge removes this misidentification by recognizing the non-difference of *ātman* and *brahman*.

Empirical plurality is not simply nonexistent within Advaita. It operates within conventional experience and is subject to causal, ethical, and epistemic distinctions. It is nevertheless secondary from the standpoint of ultimate reality, where consciousness is not divided into numerically independent selves. Advaita thus places consciousness at the center of ontology while denying that plurality is fundamental in the final analysis.

### Relation to SIO

Advaita and SIO both assign a central ontological role to subjectivity or consciousness, although they do so in different conceptual settings. Advaita ultimately resolves apparent plurality into nondual consciousness. SIO, by contrast, begins with differentiated subjectivities and treats their differentiation as a condition of constitutive intersection. Within the SIO framework, such intersection may generate an irreducible third subjectivity rather than disclose the prior identity of all subjects in a single consciousness. The comparison therefore concerns contrasting structures of nonduality and differentiation, not competing judgments about their philosophical validity.

## 5.2.2 Samkhya and Classical Yoga

Sāṃkhya articulates a dualism between *puruṣa* and *prakṛti*. *Puruṣa* is pure consciousness: inactive, non-material, and distinct from the processes it witnesses. Classical Sāṃkhya generally affirms a plurality of *puruṣas*. *Prakṛti* is primordial material nature, constituted by the three *guṇas* and capable of transformation. From it evolves a graded series of principles that includes intellect, ego, mind, sensory and motor capacities, subtle elements, and the gross material world.

Cosmological evolution begins through the proximity of *puruṣa* and *prakṛti*, but this proximity should not be described as an interaction between two active agents. *Puruṣa* does not change or causally intervene; transformation belongs to *prakṛti*. The conjunction makes experience and eventual liberation possible, while the apparent identification of consciousness with material and mental processes accounts for bondage.

Classical Yoga adopts much of this ontological framework while embedding it in a discipline of ethical restraint, concentration, and meditative practice. Its acceptance of a special *puruṣa*, Īśvara, distinguishes it from non-theistic presentations of classical Sāṃkhya without eliminating the basic difference between consciousness and nature. In both systems, liberation depends upon discriminative knowledge that distinguishes *puruṣa* from *prakṛti* and ends their mistaken identification.

### Relation to SIO

Sāṃkhya–Yoga contains differentiation, conjunction, and cosmological development. It therefore offers an important comparison for any ontology concerned with the consequences of relation between fundamentally distinct principles. No third subjectivity, however, emerges from the conjunction of *puruṣa* and *prakṛti*. *Puruṣa* remains unchanged, while the products of evolution belong to *prakṛti*. The conjunction enables experience but does not generate an irreducible subject that recursively reconstructs both originating terms.

## 5.2.3 Nyaya–Vaisheshika

Nyāya and Vaiśeṣika developed as distinct schools but became increasingly integrated into a shared realist framework. Their mature ontology classifies what exists through categories such as substance, quality, action, universal, particularity, inherence, and, in later formulations, absence. Substance (*dravya*) provides the principal locus for qualities and actions. The recognized substances include material elements, time, space, mind, and self, although lists and analyses vary across historical stages.

This ontology is both realist and pluralist. Selves are enduring substances associated with cognition, desire, pleasure, pain, and volition, rather than momentary streams or manifestations of a single consciousness. Material analysis includes eternal atoms as well as composite objects. Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika philosophers also defend the reality of wholes: a composite may be a genuine substance dependent upon, but not simply identical with, an aggregate of atoms.

Relations are indispensable to this account. Conjunction and disjunction explain changing connections, while inherence (*samavāya*) explains forms of inseparable dependence, such as those between a substance and its qualities or between a whole and its parts. Relations thus organize a complex reality and contribute to the explanation of composite formation. Yet the framework remains substance-first in the relevant respect: relations connect, qualify, or structure entities but are not ordinarily treated as sources of newly generated subjectivity.

### Relation to SIO

Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika provides an Indian analogue to substance-first ontology, although it should not be identified without qualification with Aristotelian metaphysics. Its categories, atomism, theory of wholes, and account of the self arise from distinct intellectual problems. For the present comparison, the central difference is that subjects are enduring substances rather than products of constitutive intersection. Relations may generate or organize composite wholes, but the tradition does not appear to propose that a relation generates an irreducible third subjectivity that acts back upon the selves involved.

## 5.2.4 Early Buddhism and Madhyamaka

Early Buddhist teachings reject the identification of a permanent self with any component of experience. The doctrine of no-self (*anātman*) analyzes the person through changing aggregates and denies that an enduring, autonomous self can be found within or apart from them. Dependent origination (*pratītyasamutpāda*) explains phenomena as arising in dependence upon conditions. Applied to the person, it accounts for continuity, action, and suffering without requiring an unchanging subject-substance.

The ontological implications of these teachings were developed differently by subsequent Buddhist schools. It is therefore preferable not to treat “early Buddhism” as a fully systematized relational ontology. Nevertheless, the combination of no-self and dependent origination provides a major historical alternative to theories that grant ontological priority to independent substances.

Madhyamaka, beginning with Nāgārjuna and elaborated by later interpreters, extends the critique of intrinsic nature (*svabhāva*) to all phenomena. To be empty (*śūnya*) is not to be simply nonexistent; it is to lack independent, self-grounding essence. Phenomena function conventionally through causal, conceptual, and practical dependencies, while analysis discloses no intrinsic nature that could establish them independently of those conditions. Emptiness itself is not a further substance or absolute ground. The relation between conventional and ultimate truth prevents the critique of intrinsic existence from becoming a denial of conventional efficacy.

### Relation to SIO

Early Buddhism and Madhyamaka provide some of the strongest historical resources for relational and anti-essentialist ontology, although the designation “relational ontology” is a modern interpretive description rather than a neutral translation of their own categories. Their analyses demonstrate how dependent processes can account for experience without an enduring self-substance.

The direction of analysis nevertheless differs from SIO. Buddhist arguments tend to deconstruct or redescribe enduring subjectivity rather than posit an irreducible third subject generated through the intersection of differentiated subjects. Dependent origination offers a partial analogue to constitutive dependence, but the dependently arisen process is not thereby treated as a third subjectivity with recursive agency over two originating subjects. Madhyamaka also resists assigning intrinsic ontological status to any such emergent term.

## 5.2.5 Jain Ontology

Jain philosophy affirms a pluralist realism comprising living selves (*jīvas*) and non-living realities (*ajīvas*). Selves are numerous, enduring, and individually distinct. Embodied existence results from their association with karmic matter, while liberation involves the removal of karmic obstruction rather than absorption into a single universal self.

Jain accounts of substance combine persistence and change. A real entity endures as a substance while undergoing modifications in its modes. Permanence and alteration are therefore not mutually exclusive descriptions when applied under the appropriate aspects. This framework supports *anekāntavāda*, the doctrine that reality is many-sided and cannot be exhaustively characterized by a single unqualified assertion.

The associated discipline of conditional predication does not reduce truth to arbitrary perspective. Rather, it specifies the standpoint and respect under which a claim is valid. Jain many-sided realism consequently preserves both an objective plurality of entities and a plurality of legitimate, partial descriptions. It offers a systematic alternative to strict monism, exclusive dualism, and theories that identify change with the disappearance of enduring reality.

### Relation to SIO

Jain philosophy is relevant to SIO because it grants ontological weight to plural selves, perspectival differentiation, relational conditions, persistence, and transformation. Its many-sided analysis also shows how apparently opposed descriptions may be jointly valid without being simply conflated. It does not, however, clearly propose that the constitutive intersection of two differentiated selves generates an irreducible third subject. Perspectival plurality qualifies knowledge of existing realities; it is not itself a mechanism for producing a new subjectivity that recursively reconstructs the original selves.

## 5.2.6 Kashmir Shaivism

“Kashmir Śaivism” designates a family of nondual Śaiva traditions rather than a single homogeneous school. Among its most philosophically systematic expressions is the Pratyabhijñā, or Recognition, tradition associated with Utpaladeva and Abhinavagupta. These thinkers understand ultimate reality as Śiva, not as an inert unity but as self-luminous consciousness inseparable from Śakti, its power of awareness, differentiation, manifestation, and action.

The world is a real manifestation of consciousness rather than an ontologically independent domain or a mere illusion external to Śiva. Through Śakti, universal consciousness articulates itself as subjects, objects, cognitive acts, and levels of manifestation. Finite subjectivity results from contraction or limitation, through which the universal agency of consciousness is experienced under restricted conditions.

Recognition (*pratyabhijñā*) is the realization that finite subjectivity is not ultimately separate from Śiva. This recognition transforms the subject's understanding of its identity and capacities; it does not create an identity that was previously absent. The tradition's analyses frequently employ triadic structures, including knower, knowing, and known, as well as multiple triads associated with Trika classifications. Such structures give differentiation and mediation important roles within a nondual ontology, while retaining the priority of Śiva's self-manifestation and self-recognition.

### Relation to SIO

Kashmir Śaivism is the closest Indian comparison considered in this section. It assigns ontological reality to consciousness, integrates unity with differentiated manifestation, employs triadic structures, and describes a transformation of finite subjectivity through recognition. These features justify examining it as more than a general analogy to relation or nonduality.

The structural difference remains significant. Kashmir Śaivism centers on Śiva's manifestation through Śakti and on the finite subject's recognition of an identity that is ultimately already the case. SIO, as formulated for comparison in this paper, proposes that differentiated subjectivities can enter a constitutive intersection from which an irreducible third subjectivity emerges. The third is not simply the recognition or manifestation of a pre-existing universal subject. Kashmir Śaivism may partially approximate the generative and recursive dimensions of SIO, depending on how its triadic structures are interpreted, but it does not clearly contain the complete N1–N6 conjunction. This conclusion marks a structural difference and does not imply a ranking of the two positions.

## Interim Verdict

Indian philosophy developed an extensive range of ontological alternatives concerning consciousness, subjectivity, relation, plurality, persistence, change, dependence, and nonduality. The traditions examined here include nondual consciousness ontology, dualist cosmology, substance realism, no-self and dependent origination, many-sided pluralism, and nondual theories of manifestation and recognition. These positions cannot be reduced to a common Indian ontology.

Among them, Kashmir Śaivism represents the closest historical comparison to SIO under the criteria used in this paper. Its ontological treatment of consciousness, differentiated manifestation, triadic mediation, and transformation through recognition creates several points of structural proximity. Those points do not establish equivalence.

On the evidence considered here, no single historical Indian ontology has yet been identified that integrates the complete conjunction of:

- differentiated subjectivities;
- constitutive intersection;
- an irreducible emergent third subjectivity; and
- recursive reconstruction of the originating subjectivities.

This is an interim comparative judgment rather than a comprehensive claim about the entirety of Indian philosophical literature. It remains subject to revision through more detailed examination of primary texts, commentarial traditions, and alternative scholarly interpretations.
