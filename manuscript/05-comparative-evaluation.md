5. Comparative Evaluation

## 5.1 Purpose and Analytical Scope

Chapter 4 distinguished textual evidence, interpretive reconstruction, and structural assessment for each comparison case and applied N1–N6 individually. The purpose of the present chapter is not to revise or reinterpret those assessments, but to integrate them across the comparison set and describe the structural patterns identified within the scope of the review.

Accordingly, this chapter introduces no new comparison cases, literature, or evaluation criteria. It uses only the assessments fixed in Chapter 4 to examine which structures recur across multiple traditions, which criteria are distributed across different theories, and which combinations are repeatedly absent.

The conclusions of this chapter are restricted to the cases examined in this manuscript. Failure to identify complete structural correspondence here does not establish the absence of a comparable structure from the history of thought as a whole, including philosophical, religious, or intellectual traditions not examined in this study.

N1–N6 were derived from the structure of SIO defined in Chapter 2. This chapter therefore does not reassess SIO itself under N1–N6. The question is how the ontologies examined in Chapter 4 correspond to those criteria.

## 5.3 Recurring Structures

### 5.3.1 Third Terms and Third Subjectivity

N1 asks whether a third term constituted in relation to two subjectivities is treated as a subjectivity irreducible to them. No case examined in Chapter 4 received clear satisfaction for N1.

Partial or interpretation-dependent correspondence was identified in Plotinus, Kashmir Śaivism, Trinitarian ontology, Schelling, Hegel, Nishida, Whitehead, and the Analytic Third. These theories contain triadic structures, unities constituted through relation, derivative subjects, new experiential structures, or relational organizations irreducible to the individual terms involved.

In each case, however, interpretive qualifications remain concerning whether the third term is a subjectivity in the sense defined by SIO. The existence of triadicity, mediation, unity, a relational field, or emergent organization does not by itself establish the existence of an irreducible third subjectivity. The distribution of N1 therefore confirms the need to distinguish the presence of a third term from the presence of a third subjectivity.

### 5.3.2 Generation Through Intersection

Participatory Sense-Making and the Analytic Third received clear satisfaction for N2. Both treat relation or interaction not as an external channel through which already constituted content is transmitted, but as a constitutive process through which new meaning or experience arises.

Partial correspondence with N2 was identified in early Buddhism, Madhyamaka, Kashmir Śaivism, Schelling, Hegel, Nishida, Whitehead, and Merleau-Ponty. Through dependent constitution, dynamic generation, mediation, reciprocal determination, concrescence, or chiasm, these theories indicate that what exists is not always fully constituted independently of relation.

The constitutive character of relation is nevertheless not identical to the generation of a third subjectivity through the intersection of two differentiated subjectivities. The distribution of N2—partial correspondence across several theories but clear satisfaction in only two contemporary cases—therefore supports the distinction between relation in general and the specific direction of generation through intersection.

### 5.3.3 Action upon the Originating Subjectivities

Only the Analytic Third received clear satisfaction for N3. In this case, the co-generated analytic process alters the experience and understanding of both analyst and analysand as part of the structure described by the theory.

Partial correspondence was identified in Kashmir Śaivism, Schelling, Hegel, Nishida, and Participatory Sense-Making. In these cases, manifestation, generated forms, institutions, the historical world, or an interaction process acts upon finite subjects or participants and changes their modes of organization or understanding.

Interpretive limitations remain, however, concerning whether this action proceeds from a third subjectivity constituted through intersection. Transformation, interaction, and reorganization may occur without constituting the reconstitution of originating subjectivities by a third subjectivity.

### 5.3.4 Ontological Status of the Candidate Third Term

Trinitarian ontology and Whitehead received clear satisfaction for N4. In Trinitarian ontology, the Holy Spirit is formulated as the third hypostasis possessing full divine reality. In Whitehead, the actual occasion is not merely a descriptive construction but has ontological status as a subject in becoming.

Clear satisfaction of N4 does not entail satisfaction of N1 or N2. In Trinitarian ontology, the third hypostasis is not a subjectivity newly generated through intersection. Whitehead's actual occasion is likewise not ordinarily defined as a third subjectivity arising through the intersection of two differentiated subjectivities.

Plotinus, Kashmir Śaivism, Schelling, Hegel, Nishida, the Analytic Third, and cosmopsychism received partial or interpretation-dependent satisfaction for N4. In these cases, some reality is attributed to the candidate third term or to subjectivity, but it remains interpretation-dependent which term should function as the candidate third or whether its reality extends to general ontological subjectivity.

This distribution confirms that the ontological reality of a third term must be assessed independently of both its subjective status and its direction of generation.

### 5.3.5 General Theoretical Scope

Plotinus, Kashmir Śaivism, Schelling, Hegel, Nishida, Whitehead, and cosmopsychism received clear satisfaction for N5. These theories present general structures concerning being, consciousness, nature, spirit, events, or cosmic subjectivity rather than restricting their accounts to a particular interpersonal relation or individual case.

Early Buddhism, Madhyamaka, Jain philosophy, Trinitarian ontology, Merleau-Ponty, and Participatory Sense-Making received partial satisfaction. These theories may have broad scope, but the particular structure compared in Chapter 4 is not univocally presented as a general ontology of subjectivity corresponding to SIO.

The Analytic Third, by contrast, clearly satisfied N2 and N3 but did not satisfy N5 because its domain is restricted to the psychoanalytic situation. This result illustrates that structural proximity and theoretical generality are distinct dimensions of comparison.

## 5.4 Distribution of Criteria and Recurrently Absent Conjunctions

The results of Chapter 4 do not indicate that the elements associated with N1–N5 were absent from earlier thought. Rather, those elements appear in distributed form across multiple traditions.

Triadic structures and the reality of third terms appear in Plotinus and Trinitarian ontology. Dependent constitution and constitutive relation occur in Buddhist thought, phenomenology, German Idealism, process philosophy, and Participatory Sense-Making. An experiential structure generated through relation and acting upon its participants appears with particular clarity in the Analytic Third. Structures that treat subjectivity as a general ontological principle are present in Kashmir Śaivism, German Idealism, Whitehead, and cosmopsychism.

These elements are not, however, integrated in the same way within a single theory. Chapter 4 revealed several recurrent separations.

First, where a candidate third term receives clear ontological reality, that term tends not to be generated through intersection. In Trinitarian ontology, the Holy Spirit is an eternal third hypostasis rather than something emerging from an intersection of Father and Son. In Whitehead, a new subjective unity arises through relation, but the process is not formulated as an intersection of two differentiated subjectivities and does not include the retroactive reconstitution of the originating subjects.

Second, where generation through interaction or intersection is clear, the ontological subjectivity or general scope of the generated third term tends to remain limited. In Participatory Sense-Making, interaction constitutes meaning and acts upon the participants, but the interaction process itself is not defined as a subjectivity. In the Analytic Third, the co-generated third experiential structure and its effects upon the participants are clear, but its ontological status remains interpretation-dependent and its theoretical scope is restricted to the analytic situation.

Third, theories with the scope of a general ontology do not clearly formulate at least one among intersection, third subjectivity, and action upon the originating subjectivities. Plotinus, Kashmir Śaivism, Schelling, Hegel, Nishida, Whitehead, and cosmopsychism all clearly satisfied N5, but none clearly satisfied all of N1–N4.

The principal difference across the comparison set therefore does not consist merely in the presence or absence of individual elements. What matters is how the subjective status of the third term, its direction of generation, its action upon the originating subjectivities, its ontological reality, and the theoretical scope of the structure are integrated within a single account.

## 5.5 Cross-Case Assessment of N6

N6 is not an additional criterion independent of N1–N5. It is the conjunction condition assessing whether they are integrated within a single general ontology.

None of the cases examined in Chapter 4 received clear satisfaction for N6. Kashmir Śaivism, Schelling, Hegel, and Nishida received partial or interpretation-dependent satisfaction. Every other case received non-satisfaction for N6 because at least one of N1–N5 was not satisfied.

The four cases receiving partial satisfaction for N6 share a common assessment pattern. Each received partial or interpretation-dependent satisfaction for N1–N4 and clear satisfaction for N5. Each therefore presents a general ontology with some correspondence concerning third subjectivity, generation through intersection, action upon originating subjectivities, and the ontological status of the candidate third term.

None of those correspondences, however, reached clear satisfaction for each of N1–N4. Moreover, integrating multiple phases of thought, concepts, or triadic structures into a single structure corresponding to SIO remained interpretation-dependent. Partial satisfaction of N6 therefore cannot be treated as a complete structural anticipation of SIO.

No comparison case was found in Chapter 4 to clearly satisfy all of N1–N5 and integrate them within a single general ontology.

### Proposition 5.1. Absence of Clear Structural Equivalence within the Comparative Set

Let \(\mathcal{H}\) denote the set of comparison cases examined in Chapter 4. Let N1–N5, as defined in Chapter 3, designate the structural requirements of SIO, and let N6 designate the conjunction condition assessing their integration within a single general ontology.

Under the definition established in Chapter 3, a judgment that a comparison case bears clear structural equivalence to SIO requires clear satisfaction of N1–N5 and clear satisfaction of N6 as their conjunction condition.

Under the assessments fixed in Chapter 4,

\[
\forall h\in\mathcal{H},\quad N_6(h)\neq\mathrm{Clear}.
\]

Consequently, no comparison case is identified as a clear structural equivalent to SIO within the examined set; in particular, no case clearly satisfies N1–N5 and integrates them within a single general ontology.

#### Proof

No comparison case in the criterion-level assessments of Chapter 4 received clear satisfaction for N6.

Kashmir Śaivism, Schelling, Hegel, and Nishida received partial or interpretation-dependent satisfaction for N6. Each of these cases, however, received only partial or interpretation-dependent satisfaction for N1–N4 and therefore did not clearly satisfy all of N1–N5.

Every comparison case other than these four failed to satisfy at least one of N1–N5 and was consequently assessed as not satisfying N6 under the conjunction rule established in Chapter 3.

It follows that

\[
\neg\exists h\in\mathcal{H}\quad N_6(h)=\mathrm{Clear}.
\]

Because Chapter 3 establishes clear satisfaction of N6 as a necessary condition for identifying clear structural equivalence, no clear structural equivalent to SIO is identified within the comparison set \(\mathcal{H}\). □

### Corollary 5.1. Distribution of Constituent Structures

Clear or partial correspondences to individual structures represented by N1–N5 are distributed across multiple theories within \(\mathcal{H}\). They are not, however, conjoined within a single comparison case in a form that clearly satisfies all of N1–N5.

### Corollary 5.2. Non-identification of SIO as a Merely Terminological Restatement

Within the set of cases examined in Chapter 4, no comparison case is identified as a clear structural equivalent to SIO. On the basis of the present comparison, SIO therefore cannot be identified as a merely terminological restatement of any one theory within the comparison set.

### Scope of Application

Proposition 5.1 and its corollaries are relative to the comparison cases examined in Chapter 4, the criteria defined in Chapter 3, and the textual interpretations and structural assessments fixed in Chapter 4.

They do not establish the absolute historical novelty of SIO across traditions not examined in this manuscript. Nor do they establish its philosophical superiority, truth, conceptual coherence, or empirical validity.

## 5.6 The Structural Position of SIO within the Examined Comparison Set

Proposition 5.1 establishes that the comparison set examined in Chapter 4 contains no case identified as a clear structural equivalent to SIO—that is, no case that clearly satisfies N1–N5 and integrates them within a single general ontology. Within the scope of the comparison, SIO therefore cannot be identified as a merely terminological restatement of any one previously examined theory.

This result does not deny partial or interpretation-dependent structural correspondence. The partial satisfaction of N6 by Kashmir Śaivism, Schelling, Hegel, and Nishida indicates that several structures associated with SIO may be integrated in specific ways within each of these ontologies.

What Proposition 5.1 excludes is not the existence of such correspondence, but the identification of clear structural equivalence under the assessments presently established. Partial satisfaction of N6 is therefore retained not as proof of non-equivalence, but as structural proximity requiring further textual and interpretive examination.

The synthesis does not support the conclusion that the individual elements constituting SIO lack historical precedents. Third terms, triadicity, relational constitution, emergence, reciprocal transformation, the ontological reality of subjectivity, and general ontology have each been developed in different traditions.

Within the examined comparison set, the structural position of SIO lies not in introducing each of these elements independently, but in integrating the following within a single ontological formulation:

- differentiated subjectivities;
- constitutive intersection;
- an irreducible third subjectivity arising through intersection;
- action of the third subjectivity upon the originating subjectivities;
- the ontological reality of the third subjectivity; and
- the general theoretical scope of this structure.

Neither Proposition 5.1 nor Corollary 5.2 entails that SIO is the first ontology in the history of thought, universally unique, or more adequate than other ontologies.

First, the comparison set is not exhaustive. Second, the individual assessments retain interpretive uncertainty concerning the selection of texts, reconstruction of concepts, and application of the criteria. Third, structural distinction from the examined theories does not establish the conceptual coherence, truth, or empirical validity of SIO.

The conclusion supported by this chapter is narrower. Within the comparison set examined in this manuscript, structures corresponding to N1–N5 appear in distributed form across several traditions, but no comparison case was found to clearly satisfy all of them and integrate them within a single general ontology.

In this limited sense, SIO may be situated not as a merely terminological restatement of any one examined theory, but as a distinguishable ontological research program that integrates previously distributed elements according to a specific direction of constitution and conjunction.

This positioning is a limited structural assessment derived from historical comparison, not an argument requiring the adoption of SIO. Its philosophical adequacy, conceptual coherence, formalizability, and empirical applicability must be examined independently of its structural relation to the historical comparison set.
